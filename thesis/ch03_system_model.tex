\chapter{System Model and Threat Model}\label{ch:sysmodel}

This chapter fixes what the rest of the thesis reasons about: the network, the ledger, the
traffic and its two operating points, the adversary, the design choices, and the two frame
formats. A frame as it is sent, byte by byte, is in \Cref{sec:worked}.

\Cref{fig:system} shows the system as a whole. Each UAV codes its records, chains them and
signs them into frames, and broadcasts the frames on a shared channel; each receiver decodes,
rebuilds, verifies and stores the frames of its neighbours; and five constraints, one per
equation of \Cref{ch:theory} and \Cref{ch:codesign}, select the batch, the placement of the
signature, the scheme and the key-record interval.

\section{Network}

$N$ UAVs share an IEEE 802.11 broadcast channel and all hear one another: one collision domain,
called here a \emph{neighbourhood} of $N$ nodes, in which each node has $N-1$ neighbours. (OFDM, base rate $R = \SI{6}{\mega\bit\per\second}$
for robustness; application MTU budget $M = 1500$\,B). Frames are lost independently with
probability $p$, an abstraction of fading, collisions and mobility, with $p \in \{0.02, 0.05,
0.10\}$ as a declared sensitivity range. Contention is modelled by the DCF (\Cref{ch:theory}) and
validated against NS-3 (\Cref{ch:validation}). There is no ground control station in the model: the
substrate is peer-to-peer broadcast.

Every capacity in \Cref{ch:codesign} is computed under this assumption except one family:
\Cref{sec:spread,sec:spread-swarm} place the nodes over discs of up to $1$\,km, with capture,
fading and hidden stations, and move them, to see what the assumption hides.

\subsection{Why broadcast, and why it matters}

IEEE 802.11 broadcast frames carry no acknowledgement and are never retransmitted. Three
consequences follow, and each shapes a later chapter. A broadcast receiver sees the \emph{raw}
channel error rate with none of the ARQ that hides loss on a unicast link, which is why the loss
grid is one to two orders of magnitude more pessimistic than the $99.9\%$ reliability TS~22.125
specifies for managed command-and-control links \emph{with} retransmission. Overload degrades
\emph{delivery}, never latency, because excess offered load is dropped rather than queued. And the
channel model is not Bianchi's --- \Cref{ch:validation} records that using the unicast model with
the acknowledgement removed under-predicts simulation by $16\times$.

\section{Ledger}

Each UAV maintains an append-only hash chain of its own telemetry records and stores verified
records overheard from others. A record is
\begin{gather*}
\texttt{rec} = \{\texttt{src}, \texttt{seq}, \texttt{ts}, \texttt{prev\_hash}, \texttt{payload}\}, \\
\texttt{prev\_hash} = \mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\text{previous record bytes}).
\end{gather*}
Only the owner appends to its own chain --- an owned-object model --- so tampering or equivocation
on a $(\texttt{src}, \texttt{seq})$ pair is detectable by chain verification. Finality and
witnessing semantics are out of scope; the deliverable is \emph{authenticated dissemination}, not
consensus.

A ledger is audited from the uploads alone. One pass over the frames of a mission, with the
public keys of the senders and nothing else, finds every equivocation and every frame that does
not verify, and the auditor signs a Merkle root of what it accepted. For \auUavs{} UAVs over a
flight hour, \auFrames{} frames, a planted equivocation and a planted forgery were both found,
with no false finding, in \auMinutes{} minutes of one core. Keeping the frames costs \auKept\,B
per record.

\section{Traffic and the two arrival rates}

Records are generated at rate $\Lambda_i$ per UAV. The aggregate arrival rate in a neighbourhood
of $N$ nodes is $\Lambda = \Lambda_i\,(N-1)$ --- the rate a \emph{receiver} must verify.

\begin{remark}
These are different quantities and conflating them is a real defect, recorded as such. A node
batches its \emph{own} records, so freshness uses $\Lambda_i$; it verifies \emph{everyone's}, so
the verification-throughput constraint uses $\Lambda$. Using $\Lambda_i$ for both silently models a
one-sender network and makes cross-signer aggregation look feasible at fleet sizes where the
receiver cannot keep up.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The adopted operating point}

$\Lambda_i = 50$\,rec/s at $\Dmax = \SI{100}{\milli\second}$ is adopted. It is PX4's
\texttt{MAVLINK\_MODE\_ONBOARD} companion-computer rate, read from autopilot source rather than
from a secondary summary, and it is fully compliant with 3GPP TS~22.125, clause~5.2.2, which specifies at
least $10$ messages per second and at most $\SI{100}{\milli\second}$ end-to-end latency for direct
UAV-to-UAV local broadcast.

The alternative $(20\,\text{rec/s}, \SI{250}{\milli\second})$ point is retained and reported
because it gives \emph{identical bytes}. The batch is bounded by the fill time, $b/\Lambda_i \leq
\Dmax$, so only the product $\Lambda_i \Dmax$ matters, and it is $5$ at both points. Five records
would fill in exactly $\Dmax$ and leave no time for the channel or for verification, so the
largest batch that meets the deadline is $b = 4$ at both (\Cref{sec:freshness-convention}), and
the frame is the same.

\subsection{The floor of the region, which is a real constraint on the application}

Holding $b \geq 4$ under a $\SI{100}{\milli\second}$ deadline requires $\Lambda_i > 40$\,Hz. At
$20$ or $10$\,Hz the same deadline forces $b = 1$ and nothing is left of the saving but the
choice of encoding. \textbf{The batching benefit is not available at every compliant operating
point}: it
requires a telemetry rate fast enough to fill a batch inside the deadline. More structurally, any
batching at all ($b \geq 2$) needs $\Lambda_i > 2/\Dmax$ --- at the standard's
$\SI{100}{\milli\second}$ bound, twice the standard's own minimum message rate. At an ArduPilot Copter default of $0$\,Hz,
where streams are requested by a ground station rather than pushed, this thesis's mechanism does
not apply at all.

\section{Threat model}\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{The adversary}

The adversary controls the radio. It can read, inject, replay, modify, delay and drop frames, and
may operate any number of radios of its own. It does not hold any honest UAV's private key; key
compromise is out of scope by charter.

\begin{remark}[What the receiver keeps, and what it did not]
The last two rows are properties of the frame on air. They become evidence only where the frame
is kept: one signature covers a frame, so a record cannot be checked without the other records
of its frame and the signature. Until 2026-10-09 the receiver of this prototype kept verified
records and discarded the frame; the pair it retained on an equivocation was a pair of records,
and nothing it held could be checked by anyone else. A review of the paper found it, and a test
that had passed throughout: it counted the pairs and never asked whether one could be checked.

The receiver now keeps every accepted frame as it arrived, and both frames of an equivocation.
Two functions are what a third party runs: one returns the records of a frame that decodes and
verifies under the sender's public key with nothing else, the other says whether two such
frames give one sequence number two different records. Neither reads any state of the receiver.
The cost is storage: a frame's length on air for every $b$ records held, \bprLeanDesign\,B per
record in the design, beside the record itself. A frame that is coded against its predecessor
cannot be shown alone; the design sends a key record in every frame, so every frame can.

Fixing this found a second defect. The receiver offered a frame's records to the store in order
and let the first refusal decide the frame. A frame whose first record repeated one already
held was therefore refused as a replay, and a different record signed for a later sequence
number in the same frame was never seen. Every record of a verified frame is now compared with
what is held before any is stored.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Formal analysis, and the evidence for each property}\label{sec:formal}

The frame protocol is modelled in the Tamarin prover~\cite{tamarin}: a sender that signs
frames with increasing sequence numbers and a link to its previous record, a receiver that
accepts in the order of \Cref{sec:protocol}, and an attacker that is the network. Origin and
integrity, the rejection of replays, their combination as injective agreement, the evidence of
an equivocation (which implies a revealed key) and the detection of a skipped frame are proven
for unboundedly many frames in a model without the chain and the clock. Continuity is proven in
a model with the chain. The time window is shown in a model with the clock: without it, a frame
held back by the attacker is accepted, and with it no such trace exists.

Three models, not one. A single model with the chain and the clock did not terminate within a
gigabyte of memory per lemma. The registered model settled three of its nine lemmas; the counters
were then keyed by a session identifier and the receiver given a start rule, and both changes
are recorded with the registration (\Cref{app:prereg}). That the properties carry from the
smaller models to the full protocol is an argument --- the smaller model's receiver checks less
and so accepts a superset --- and not a proof. Canonical encoding is assumed injective.

\subsection{What is not claimed}

Each of these is outside the design, not an oversight in it.

\begin{description}
\item[Confidentiality.] Telemetry is broadcast in clear.
\item[Availability.] Jamming removes frames, and nothing here recovers them: there is no
      retransmission and no coding across frames. Loss appears as the \emph{robustness} measure
      $V$, not as a security property. A flood is resisted only from outside the swarm: a member
      holding the group key can still make a receiver verify forged frames, and at
      \flInsiderRate{} of them a second one core no longer keeps up (\Cref{sec:flooding}).
\item[Timeliness without GNSS time.] The window refuses a held-back frame only while sender and
      receiver share GNSS time. A frame delayed by less than $\Delta$ is accepted, authentic and
      in order, and so is a later one once GNSS time has been lost for longer than the window
      allows: two clocks part by \rpDrift\,ms an hour (\Cref{sec:window}).
\item[Truthfulness.] A UAV holding a valid key can sign false telemetry. Only
      \emph{inconsistent} falsehood --- two records for one sequence number --- is detectable.
\item[Key distribution, revocation and admission.] Receivers are assumed to hold each sender's
      public key. The on-air cost of delivering certificates is charged separately
      (\Cref{ch:codesign}); who may join and how a key is withdrawn is out of scope, and so is
      Sybil resistance.
\item[Privacy.] Senders carry a stable identifier. The certificateless aggregate schemes of the
      vehicular literature buy conditional privacy that this design does not offer.
\end{description}

\subsection{Behaviour under loss}

Every frame of the design decodes alone (\Cref{thm:t3prime}), so a lost frame costs its own
records and nothing else. The receiver then holds a chain with a gap: the next frame's link
commits to a record it does not have, which it records and cannot check. Continuity across the
gap is therefore \emph{provable later}, if the missing records are ever obtained, and
\emph{unknown until then} --- neither violated nor verified.

\begin{remark}
Availability under loss is a \emph{robustness} metric in this thesis, not a security claim. The
quantity $V$, the probability that a record is verifiable at a receiver, is treated throughout as a
design constraint, not as a guarantee against an adversary who controls the channel.
\end{remark}

\section{The decision space}

\subsection{Authentication placements}

\begin{description}
\item[A --- inline per record.] Every record carries its own signature. The baseline.
\item[B --- self-batch.] One UAV packs $b$ of its \emph{own} records into a frame and signs the
      frame once. Per-record authentication cost $(\ga + \Hf)/b$. Self-contained, hence loss-local.
\item[C --- cross-signer aggregate.] A frame carries $b$ records from \emph{different}
      originators, each originally signed; the signatures aggregate into one. Required only when
      forwarding others' records or combining attestations.
\item[D --- block-level aggregate.] One signature over $b$ records spanning $n > 1$ frames.
      All-or-nothing under loss; \Cref{ch:theory} shows when this is dominated.
\end{description}

\subsection{Schemes and encodings}

Signature schemes are ECDSA-P256 ($64$\,B, legacy baseline), Ed25519 ($64$\,B, fast) and BLS12-381.
The BLS object is $96$\,B, not the $48$\,B minimum-signature variant originally specified: the
available library ships only the minimum-pubkey scheme. That deviation is recorded and applied
consistently, and it makes BLS lose the byte comparison outside multi-signer aggregation.

Encodings are JSON (baseline), canonical CBOR, MessagePack, and delta-CBOR. In the record-linked format
the delta encoder emits a key record every $K = 16$ records, a value that was fixed and never
optimised. The frame-linked format makes the interval a parameter --- one self-contained frame in every
$r$ --- and \Cref{sec:e7} evaluates it; the design uses $r = 1$.

\section{Two frame formats}\label{sec:formats}

The thesis reports two formats side by side, and the reason is historical. The \emph{first}
format is the one implemented at the start: canonical CBOR with text keys, and in every record its
own chain link, sender identifier and sequence number. It is frozen, and every artifact of
chapters~\ref{ch:bytes}--\ref{ch:validation} that predates October 2026 was produced with it. The
\emph{lean} format was built after an external review pointed out that the design the results
headlined --- delta records under one signature in a short header --- had never existed as one
object: it was a sum of sizes measured in three places (\Cref{ch:reproducibility}). The frame-linked
format is that design as code, with a sender and a receiver. The sizes reported for its design
and its baselines are lengths of frames that were emitted, decoded and verified; the one frame-linked
size that is a sum of measured parts (the design without delta coding) is marked in
\Cref{tab:ladder}.

\subsection{The frame-linked frame}

\input{fig_frame}

\Cref{fig:frame} draws the frame to scale. A frame is one canonical CBOR map with integer keys. \texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq} name the
sender and the sequence number of the first record; \texttt{link} is the hash of the record
before it; \texttt{recs} is the record stream and \texttt{auth} one signature. A record in the
stream is nine variable-length integers --- timestamp, position, velocity, battery, mode. The
first record of a frame is a \emph{key record} carrying absolute values; each later one is a
\emph{delta} from the record before it. Sender and sequence number are not repeated per record.

\textbf{What is signed is not the stream.} It is the canonical ledger records, each with its own
\texttt{prev\_hash}, exactly as in the record-linked format. The receiver rebuilds them --- the first
\texttt{prev\_hash} is \texttt{link}, each later one the hash of the record just rebuilt --- and
only then checks the signature. A frame that cannot be decoded therefore cannot be verified, and
\Cref{thm:t3prime} counts that. The state against which a later frame would be decoded advances
only after a frame has been verified \emph{and} stored, so a replayed frame cannot desynchronise
a stream.

\textbf{What one link per frame gives up.} In the record-linked format every record carries its own
\texttt{prev\_hash}; in the frame-linked format only the first record of a frame does, and the rest are
derived. Inside a frame, order and content then rest on the frame signature alone, where the record-linked
format has a signature and an independent hash. A frame is signed and accepted as a unit, so no
strength is lost --- but one mechanism remains where there were two. The decision of July 2026
had scoped per-frame chaining to the low-rate arm; the frame-linked format extends it to both, and that
extension is recorded as a decision, not presented as a neutral encoding choice.

\section{The frame header, which is a range}

$\Hf$ feeds the batching theory, the exclusion bound, the batch bounds, the channel constraint and
the energy model. It is measured from the implemented wire formats:
\[
\Hf = \operatorname{len}(\text{frame}) - \operatorname{len}(\text{records}) - \operatorname{len}(\text{link field}) - \operatorname{len}(\text{signature}),
\]
the link term being zero in the record-linked format, where the link lives inside each record.
