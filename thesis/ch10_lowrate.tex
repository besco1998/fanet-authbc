\chapter{The Low-Rate Regime and the Exclusion Bound}\label{ch:lowrate}

\section{Why the low-rate arm is a different regime, not a slower one}

The 802.11 numbers do not transfer. They differ by two to four orders of magnitude, and the
binding constraint differs \emph{in kind}: a regulatory airtime quota instead of a frame size or a
latency budget. This is the third regime in the taxonomy of \Cref{thm:t2a}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
regime & what caps the batch & where \\
\midrule
MTU-limited & frame size & --- \\
freshness-limited & $\Lambda_i \Dmax$ & \textbf{802.11} \\
\textbf{duty-cycle-limited} & regulatory airtime quota & \textbf{low-rate} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

On this arm the arrival rate and the freshness are \emph{derived}, not configured: a frame of
airtime $T$ may repeat only every $T/\text{duty}$, so the sustainable rate and the staleness of the
oldest record are the same interval. There is no independent deadline to trade against bytes.

This chapter is \emph{analysis and simulation, not measurement}. No LoRa hardware was
instrumented and no LoRa energy figure is reported: the only metered radio power in this work is a
Wi-Fi figure, and reusing it for a different transceiver would be fabrication. Every LoRa constant
is transcribed from the SX1276 datasheet~\cite{semtech_sx1276} and from LoRaWAN
RP002-1.0.3~\cite{lora_rp002} (Tables~12 and~13; EU863-870 duty cycle below $1\%$).

\section{The exclusion}\label{sec:excl}

\Cref{thm:t6} says that a link of maximum payload $M$ admits a frame that verifies alone only if
\[ M \;\geq\; \Hf + L + \ga + s_k , \]
a header, one chain link, an authentication object and one record that decodes without its
predecessor. An exclusion is claimed against the \emph{smallest frame the format can emit at
all}, not against the frames one telemetry stream happened to produce.

\subsection{The floor of the format}

Take the lean format of \Cref{ch:sysmodel} with sender~0, sequence number~0 and one record in which
every field is zero. Every integer then occupies its minimum width:
\[ \underbrace{\exFloorHdr}_{\Hf} + \underbrace{\linkField}_{L} + \underbrace{64}_{\ga}
   + \underbrace{\exFloorRec}_{s_k} = \exFloor \text{ bytes.} \]
That frame was emitted, signed and verified; it is not an estimate. \textbf{Header, link and
signature alone are \exFloorNoRec\,B.} The first format's floor is higher still, because its
record carries its own $32$\,B link and its keys are text.

DR8--DR11 are LR-FHSS. (Earlier versions of this chapter considered seven.)

\textbf{\exclCountCap{} of twelve data rates cannot carry one signed, hash-chained frame.} None
of the eight depends on the telemetry: each is excluded against the floor. They fall into two
groups that are excluded for different reasons, and the difference decides what could ever
rescue them.

\paragraph{Five rates: the signature.} At $50$ or $51$\,B a $64$\,B signature does not fit. This
holds with a zero-byte header, no chain link and no record. Only a smaller authentication object
could change it.

\paragraph{Three rates: what a frame must contain.} At $115$\,B the signature fits, and so does
the header. What does not fit is the chain link together with a record:
$\exFloorHdr + \linkField + 64 = \exFloorNoRec$, the whole payload, before a single byte of
telemetry.

\begin{remark}[Which payload column]
Published EU868 tables differ. Klimiashvili \emph{et al.} list DR3 at $123$\,B where this work
uses $115$\,B~\cite{klimiashvili2020loravswifi}. Both read the standard correctly: $123$ is the
maximum \emph{MACPayload} and $115$ the maximum \emph{application} payload, the two differing by
the $8$\,B of \texttt{FHDR} and \texttt{FPort}. The application figure is the applicable one, since
the frame travels inside \texttt{FRMPayload}. Under the other reading the floor of \exFloor\,B
still does not fit, by one byte --- too thin a margin to lean on, which is why it is recorded here
and not offered as a second argument.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Scope}

The exclusion is a statement about a particular kind of system. It holds under four conditions,
and each is necessary.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The limits are LoRaWAN's.} They come from the regional parameters. The LoRa
      physical layer itself carries $255$\,B at any spreading factor and is not excluded; a
      private LoRa network that ignores the regional payload table is outside this result.
\item \textbf{Every frame carries a public-key signature.} A symmetric tag is far smaller, and
      authenticates a link instead of an originator.
\item \textbf{There is no loss recovery.} The Remote ID authentication standard takes the
      other road --- it pages a signature over several small frames and adds a parity page
      (\Cref{sec:bg-drip}) --- and so lies outside this result by construction.
      Retransmission or coding across frames changes what it
      means for a frame to arrive.
\item \textbf{$\varepsilon \leq p$.} This is what forbids spreading a signature over several
      frames: a unit spanning $n$ frames verifies with probability $(1-p)^n$, so
      $V \geq 1-\varepsilon$ needs $n \leq \lfloor \ln(1-\varepsilon)/\ln(1-p) \rfloor$, which is
      $1$ exactly when $\varepsilon \leq p$. At $p = 0.01$ and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ a signed unit
      may span five frames, and the exclusion no longer holds as stated.
\end{enumerate}

Three readings of \Cref{tab:t6relax} are worth stating.

\textbf{The 115\,B rates are excluded by the chain link, not by the signature.} Remove the link
from the air and they fit with ordinary $64$\,B signatures. That row is arithmetic, not an
implemented format: without the link a receiver that has missed a frame cannot rebuild the next
one, so removing it trades the exclusion for the dependence that \Cref{thm:t3prime} counts.

\textbf{A three-byte miss is not an exclusion.} The smallest standardised signature is a $48$\,B
BLS12-381 point in the minimal-signature-size variant~\cite{cfrg_bls}, which targets $126$-bit
security. It fits a $51$\,B payload and leaves three bytes. At $115$\,B a frame of zeros would
fit (\exFloorBls\,B) while the smallest frame of this telemetry is \exLoBls\,B. That margin is
within reach of a format change or a different payload, so the artifact grades it separately and
it is \emph{not} counted among the excluded rates.

\textbf{At 50 or 51\,B, hash chaining itself does not fit.} Header and link are
\exHdrLinkLo--\exHdrLinkHi\,B before any authenticator or record. Even a $13$\,B symmetric tag, as
MAVLink~2 uses, leaves no room. The exclusion there rests on two independent facts.

\subsection{How this result reached its present form}\label{sec:excl-history}

This section exists because the result was stated wrongly twice, and the second error was made
\emph{while correcting the first}.

\paragraph{First statement (July 2026).} $\smax = M - \Hf - \ga \geq \smin$, with $\Hf = 44$\,B,
$\ga = 64$\,B and $\smin = 13$\,B, applied to seven data rates. DR0--DR2 were excluded by the
signature and DR3 ``by six bytes'' ($7$\,B of room for a $13$\,B record): \emph{four of seven}.

\paragraph{First correction (August 2026).} The header is $29$\,B of CBOR text key names; the
same seven keys as small integers cost $7$\,B. An integer-keyed header of $22$\,B gave DR3
$29$\,B of room, and the headline was changed to \emph{three of seven}, with DR3 ``rescued'' by a
header redesign. The correction was presented, in this chapter and in the paper's abstract, as an
example of composing two facts that had been recorded separately.

\paragraph{Second correction (October 2026).} An external review asked what a frame that
verifies alone must actually carry. Two things had never been charged in either statement:

\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{chain link}. Per-frame chaining (\Cref{sec:perframe}) moves the $32$\,B link
      out of each record and into the frame, where it still has to travel. The test had
      subtracted it from the record and added it nowhere.
\item a \textbf{record that decodes alone}. $13$\,B was the \emph{mean delta} record less its
      link. A frame that verifies without its predecessor starts with a keyframe.
\end{itemize}

With both charged, the ``$29$\,B of room'' is $29 - 35 < 0$. DR3 is excluded under either header,
whatever the telemetry. And the count had been of seven data rates where the standard defines
twelve.

\begin{remark}[What the second error teaches that the first did not]
The first correction ended with the sentence: \emph{a register that stores facts separately does
not compose them; only re-derivation does.} Re-derivation was not enough either. Every number in
the first correction was re-derived and every one was right. What was wrong was that no frame of
the proposed kind had ever been \emph{built}: the design was a sum of sizes measured in three
places. The lean format now exists as code (\Cref{ch:sysmodel}), the floor above is the length of a
frame it emitted, and the question ``does it fit'' is answered by trying.
\end{remark}

The half that was right throughout: DR0--DR2 are excluded by the signature alone.

\section{Per-frame chaining, and what it is worth}\label{sec:perframe}

On 802.11 freshness binds, so the batch is fixed by $\Lambda_i \Dmax$ and saving payload bytes
buys no extra records. Here the regional payload limit binds, so bytes convert directly into
records per frame, and the duty cycle converts records per frame into sustainable rate. Carrying
the chain link once per frame instead of once per record is therefore worth far more on this arm.

and the same change buys no extra records. The lean format carries one link per frame on both
arms. What that gives up is stated in \Cref{ch:sysmodel}: inside a frame, order and content rest on
the frame signature alone instead of on a signature and an independent hash.

\section{Capacity}

Time on air and the duty cycle are deterministic, so simulating them would be circular. One
quantity is not derivable that way --- how many nodes can share the channel --- because LoRaWAN
uplinks are pure ALOHA, with no carrier sense and no acknowledgement. Simulating LoRaWAN
scalability in ns-3 is established practice~\cite{vandenabeele2017scalability}, including
proposals to replace the ALOHA MAC with carrier sense~\cite{to2018loracsma} and extensions to
non-terrestrial links~\cite{traspadini2025lorantn}.

\subsection{A topology caveat}

LoRaWAN is by specification a star of stars with no peer-to-peer mode: a FANET survey notes that
it ``presents some limitations regarding its star topology, its medium access control (MAC) layer
and its lack of routing procedures''~\cite{paredes2023lorafanet}, and published peer-to-peer LoRa
meshes are built by discarding the LoRaWAN MAC entirely, ``not relying on LoRaWAN\ldots without
the use of gateways''~\cite{berto2021loramesh}. A LoRa FANET that was actually flown reports the
same constraint, and hybrid LoRa/802.11s UAV meshes reach for a second radio for the same
reason~\cite{davoli2021hybridlora}; having built one, Zirak \emph{et al.} converge on a single
channel and on dedicated transmit and receive radios to work around
half-duplex~\cite{zirak2021swarm}, which is the configuration modelled here. A review of mesh
proposals finds that even those pursuing flexible topologies keep ``a predominant nodes
$\rightarrow$ gateway/sink data flow''~\cite{centelles2021beyondstar}; concrete multi-hop
deployments keep the same sink-directed shape~\cite{abrardo2019multihoplora,jiang2020hybridlpwan},
and a 2025 ns-3 LoRa mesh study states that ``there is currently no standardised and
commercialised multi-hop LoRa-based network''~\cite{durand2025loramesh}.

The scenario is therefore $N$ end devices reporting to one receiver, where the 802.11 arm is a
broadcast among peers. What transfers is the part that matters: collision statistics at a
receiver depend on how many transmitters are concurrently in range, not on what the receiver is
attached to. It is also why a single demodulator is the right provisioning here and not, as it
would be for a gateway, an artificially harsh one: a UAV peer has one radio. Two ad hoc effects
remain outside the model. A half-duplex radio cannot receive during its own $364$\,ms frame,
costing a peer about $2\%$ of every other peer's frames. And $V$ is a per-receiver measure, as on
the 802.11 arm: a record reaching \emph{every} peer in one broadcast has probability about
$(1-p)^{N-1}$, which is not what $V$ measures and would need gossip or relay.

\subsection{The result, and how it must be quoted}

Simulating the frame at DR5 under the $0.95$ threshold gives $\Nmax = 3$, with a sharp cliff:
delivery is $0.960$ at $N{=}3$ and $0.917$ at $N{=}5$. There is no backoff to absorb contention.

\begin{remark}[Never quote this bare]
The bootstrap interval is $[2, 3]$ --- a knife edge --- and at the certified $N = 3$, \textbf{nine of
thirty seeds fail the very criterion being certified}. Under a per-realisation reading, where at
least $95\%$ of runs must individually meet $V$, the answer is \textbf{1}, not 3.

Both are computed and emitted. The decision to headline the mean is recorded as a decision, and it
was safe to take only because the comparative result survives either reading.
\end{remark}

An earlier value of $\Nmax = 5$ came from a three-seed sample against a bimodal distribution and is
retracted; the artifact that produced it is retained, renamed and superseded.

The capacity runs sent a $218$\,B frame of six records on the $222$\,B payload table, which is the
table the simulator enforces. The corrected frames on that table are $214.0$\,B (lean, six
records) and $221.7$\,B (first format, four records), within $2\%$ of it in size and airtime, so
the runs were not repeated.

\subsection{Why DR5 and not DR6}

\Cref{tab:t6} admits DR4, DR5 and DR6 equally, and DR6 (SF7 at $250$\,kHz) runs at twice DR5's bit
rate, so the choice needs stating. \emph{Capacity does not change.} At a fixed duty cycle $d$ the
transmissions falling inside one $2T_{\text{air}}$ vulnerability window number
$N \cdot 2T_{\text{air}}/T_{\text{period}} = 2Nd$: \textbf{the airtime cancels}, so
$P(\text{deliver}) = e^{-2(N-1)d}$ is rate-independent and $\Nmax$ is the same at either rate.
That closed form reproduces the measured DR5 run to within $0.9$ percentage points across
$N{=}2$--$10$ and returns $\Nmax=3$, matching the simulation. DR6 therefore doubles the aggregate
at the same three nodes, and pays for it in link budget: $-120$ against $-123$\,dBm is $3$\,dB,
or $1.41\times$ less range under free-space spreading.

\textbf{This is derived and not simulated, and the reason is a limitation worth recording.}
The module's sensitivity vectors are indexed by \emph{spreading factor alone} --- there is no
bandwidth dimension in \texttt{EndDeviceLoraPhy::SENSITIVITY} or its gateway counterpart, and
none in the interference helper. Forcing SF7 onto a $250$\,kHz channel would have returned DR6's
doubled bit rate while silently keeping DR5's noise floor, so the $3$\,dB penalty --- DR6's
entire cost --- would have been invisible in a run that completed cleanly and repeated across all
30 seeds.

\subsection{What the configuration models, and what it does not}

$\Nmax = 3$ is far below the node counts usually quoted for LoRaWAN, and the difference is
configuration. The simulation uses the LoRa physical layer on its harshest MAC provisioning: a
single logical channel, a single demodulation path and one forced spreading factor, since the
data rate is a design variable here and each run fixes it. The interference matrix treats
\emph{any} same-spreading-factor overlap as fatal, so there is no capture, where a real receiver
recovers the stronger of two colliding frames. Enabling a $6$\,dB capture threshold raises
delivery by $2.7$ points at $N{=}8$ and by $1.29\times$ at $N{=}50$ without moving $\Nmax$. The
small margin over the Poisson curve $e^{-2G}$ at low load is traffic regularity, not capture:
sources are periodic, for which the escape probability is $(1-2\tau/T)^{N-1} = 0.868$ at $N{=}8$
against $0.871$ measured.

\subsection{The result is range-limited, and that limit binds first}

The channel model attributes all loss to collisions. Enabling correlated shadowing changes the
outcome on no row at either $500$ or $1000$\,m, and that is a property of the link budget: with
$14$\,dBm transmit power and $-130$\,dBm SF7 gateway sensitivity, log-distance path loss leaves
$28.8$\,dB of margin at $500$\,m and $17.5$\,dB at $1000$\,m, which a shadowing standard
deviation of about $8$\,dB cannot erode. \emph{The propagation parameters describe a considerably
better link than a real drone-to-drone channel.} Field measurements of air-to-air LoRa on real
drones~\cite{zirak2021swarm} show a link losing frames to range alone: delivery falls from
$0.9711$ at $200$\,m to $0.9550$ at $500$\,m and $0.9045$ at $1000$\,m, measured with two drones
and a base station, where contention is negligible. The two loss mechanisms are independent, so
delivery is $P_{\text{link}}(d) \times P_{\text{no-coll}}(N)$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
$V$ target & $200$\,m & $500$\,m & $1000$\,m \\
\midrule
$0.95$ & 1 & 1 & 0 \\
$0.90$ & 3 & 3 & 1 \\
$0.85$ & 5 & 5 & 3 \\
$0.80$ & 10 & 8 & 5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{At $V \geq 0.95$ the regime admits no multi-node network at all.} The link alone
delivers $0.9711$ at $200$\,m, leaving $0.0039$ of headroom, while the least-loaded measured point
($N{=}2$) already spends $0.0283$ on collisions. Relaxing to $V \geq 0.90$ restores $\Nmax = 3$,
and to $0.85$ restores $5$. The curve is reported instead of a point because the choice of
threshold, not the physics, decides whether this regime is usable, and that choice belongs to the
deployment. \textbf{$\Nmax=3$ is therefore a worst-case bound at short range --- one channel, one
demodulator, one spreading factor, $d \leq 500$\,m --- and not a LoRaWAN network capacity.}

\subsection{Mobility}\label{sec:lora-mobility}

The capacity runs keep the nodes still. Whether that matters was tested: twenty nodes sending
the $218$\,B frame every $36.6$\,s within $1000$\,m, static and under three mobility models,
thirty seeds each. The runs use the interference matrix that allows capture, because without
capture position cannot enter a collision's outcome at all, and the senders' random streams are
pinned so that installing a mobility model does not change what the senders draw.

\begin{center}
\begin{adjustbox}{max width=\textwidth}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule

\midrule

Gauss--Markov, $20$\,m/s     & 0.7451 & 0.066 & $-0.03$\,pp & $967$\,m \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{adjustbox}
\end{center}

No arm differs from the static one by more than $0.06$ of the standard deviation between runs
($|t| \leq 0.22$), although nodes move most of a kilometre. Without capture the four arms are
identical run for run, as they must be. The reason is the link margin of the previous
subsection: motion inside the simulated area never brings a link near the sensitivity threshold,
so the only route left is the power ratio of two colliding frames, and over thirty seeds that
averages out. The two mobility models are indistinguishable here, so the choice between them
carries no weight in this result.

This says that mobility does not change \emph{collision-limited} delivery in this
configuration. It does not say mobility is harmless to a link: Doppler, antenna pattern and a
node that leaves range are outside the model. And the comparison is only as good as its
control: before the random streams were pinned it showed a five-point penalty for motion that
was produced entirely by the simulator handing the senders different streams once a mobility
model had been installed (class C4 of \Cref{ch:reproducibility}).

\subsection{What infrastructure collection would buy}

The complementary question --- what if a ground gateway collected the ledger --- is a
configuration change: three channels and eight demodulation paths, with the same forced data
rate. Delivery improves substantially and the improvement grows with load, reaching
$1.90\times$ at $N{=}50$ ($0.3755 \rightarrow 0.7134$) and still $0.5071$ at $N{=}100$.
\textbf{Yet $\Nmax$ moves only from 3 to 8} (95\% interval $[5,8]$; the bare value must not be
quoted). The $V \geq 0.95$ criterion sits on a near-vertical part of the delivery curve --- the
gateway passes at $N{=}8$ with $0.9526$ and fails at $N{=}10$ with $0.9398$ --- so a threefold
reduction in offered load per channel moves the threshold barely at all.

\begin{remark}[Two cautions about that comparison]
Their $\Nmax$ is decided at $N = 4$ and $N = 5$, both inside the region their own fit is
intercept-dominated: the polynomial predicts $1.78\%$ loss at \emph{zero} transmitters, roughly
$40\%$ of the entire loss budget the criterion allows. Forcing the fit through the origin would give
them $\Nmax = 5$, \emph{widening} the gap --- so quoting $4$ is the conservative choice.

And the pessimism ratio is not a single number. It runs $0.91\times$ at $N = 2$, where \emph{this
work is the more optimistic model}, through $1.07\times$ at $N = 3$, to $2.1\times$ from $N = 10$
upward. The sign changes in exactly the region where $\Nmax$ is decided. An earlier finding in this
project quoted a single ratio in the wrong direction and had to be retracted.
\end{remark}

\section{What the regime is for}

Three nodes at about $0.17$ records per second each are \loraAgg{} records per second in all,
where the 802.11 design carries some \wifiAgg. The low-rate link is not a slow 802.11. Its
defensible role for a ledger is long-range \emph{provenance} --- a tamper-evident trickle --- and
not live telemetry. An independent ns-3 comparison of the two technologies reaches the same
structural conclusion from a different direction: Klimiashvili \emph{et al.} find neither
