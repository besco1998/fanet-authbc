This is \Cref{thm:t1} instantiated: at delta encoding the signature is \emph{larger} than the record
it signs. The motivating claim of the thesis is a measurement, not a conjecture.

\begin{remark}[What $s$ contains]
Every size in \Cref{tab:e1} includes the record's own chain link: $32$\,B in the binary
encodings, $64$ hexadecimal characters in JSON. The delta record is $13$\,B of telemetry and
$32$\,B of link, so the link is $71\%$ of what the table calls the record. Counted with the
signature, authentication material is $96$ of $109$\,B, $88\%$, not $58.7\%$: $\varphi$ as
defined understates the point it was introduced to make. An external review made this
observation, and it is what the lean format acts on (\Cref{sec:e6}): the link is carried once
per frame.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$s$ is not a property of the encoding alone]
The sizes above are measured over the first $\sim$50 seconds of each simulated flight. Sequence
numbers and timestamps grow with the record index, and variable-length encodings charge for the
extra digits, so JSON, CBOR and MessagePack all grow by 2--4\,B over a ten-times-longer window
while delta-CBOR stays flat --- it encodes differences. The bootstrap interval quoted for CBOR is
$\pm0.02$\,B and describes \emph{seed} variation; the systematic dependence on window length is
$\approx 2.7$\,B, roughly $150\times$ wider. The direction is conservative: a longer flight inflates
the baseline and leaves the optimum alone, so the reported saving is the pessimistic end.
\end{remark}

\section{E2 --- batching is the cure, and which ceiling binds}

Sweeping placement A against B across encodings and MTU budgets confirms \Cref{prop:t2}
qualitatively and \Cref{thm:t2a} quantitatively. At $M = 256$ the MTU binds and amplification is
operative; at $M = 576$ and $M = 1500$ freshness binds and the marginal value of compression is
$1.0000$ to twelve decimal places.

\textbf{The practical consequence.} On 802.11 the amplification law that motivates aggressive
compression is never operative. Compression still pays --- it pays exactly $1\times$.

\section{E3 --- the loss frontier}

Measured verifiability against batch size and loss probability, placement B versus D. Frame-level
batching Pareto-dominates block-level for all $V > (1-p)^2$, and at $\varepsilon = p$ block-level is
infeasible beyond one frame, as \Cref{thm:t3} requires.

\begin{remark}[What this experiment does and does not validate]
The emulator implements independent per-frame loss, which is also what the closed form assumes.
Agreement is therefore a \emph{consistency check}, not a validation of the loss model. What rescues
the ordering is \Cref{thm:t3c}, which holds for any stationary loss process, plus Gilbert--Elliott
simulation at matched mean rate showing $V_D$ approaching but never reaching $1-p$.
\end{remark}

\section{E10 --- loss with the decoder in the loop}\label{sec:e7}

E3 compares one-frame placements with a block that spans frames. It does not exercise the case
\Cref{thm:t3prime} describes, because in E3 every frame of placement B is assumed to verify alone.
With delta coding that is true only of a frame that starts with a keyframe.

E10 removes the assumption by removing the model: records go through the lean sender, a loss
process, and the lean receiver, and $V$ is the fraction of generated records that the receiver
decoded, verified and stored. Thirty seeds of \lossFrames{} frames per cell in all.

\centering

The design as first published is the $r = 4$ row: \lossVFour, not $0.95$. The theorem predicts
the measurement in every row, and at every other loss rate tried ($p$ from $2.3\times10^{-4}$ to
$0.10$). With bursty loss of the same mean --- a Gilbert process with bursts of four frames ---
the $r = 4$ design verifies \lossVFourBurst: better, and still short.

\begin{remark}[What this experiment validates, and what it does not]
Unlike E3, agreement here is not a tautology: the closed form counts frames, while the experiment
runs a codec and a receiver that know nothing of the formula, and they agree. What it does not
validate is the loss process itself, which is synthetic at the frame level in both.
\end{remark}

The corrected design uses $r = 1$. It costs \lossPrize\,B per record more than $r = 16$ would,
and it is the only interval that meets the target at the design's own loss rate.

\subsection{When loss grows with frame length}

Everything above gives every frame the same loss probability. At a fixed bit error rate a frame
of $L$ bytes with $o$ bytes of MAC overhead survives with probability $(1-\beta)^{8(L+o)}$, so
two frames on one channel are related by
\[
p(L) = 1 - (1 - p_{\mathrm{ref}})^{(L+o)/(L_{\mathrm{ref}}+o)} ,
\]
and a batch, being a longer frame, is lost more often than the one-record frame it replaces.
E10 has a third loss process for this: the bit error rate is set so that the \emph{one-record}
frame of the lean format is lost with probability $p$, and each frame is then lost by its own
length.

On a channel that loses $5\%$ of one-record frames, the design's frame is lost with probability
\lossBerP, and \lossBerTh{} of its records are expected to verify (measured:
\lossBerV~\lossBerCi). \textbf{That is below the target the design meets at equal frame
loss.} It reaches $0.95$ only while one-record frames are lost at most \lossBerPmax{} of the
time. The loss budget $p = \varepsilon$ had no margin (\Cref{ch:theory}); under this reading
the batch spends margin it does not have. The byte and capacity comparisons of
\Cref{ch:codesign} are at equal frame loss and carry this qualification.

\section{E9 --- what a frame is made of}\label{sec:e6}

\Cref{tab:fields} gives the byte budget of both formats. Three things in it matter for
everything downstream, and none was visible while sizes were quoted as single numbers.

\paragraph{A delta is not a constant of the encoder.} It encodes a difference, and a difference
grows with the time it spans:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
spacing between records & 50\,ms & 0.1\,s & 1\,s & 5.5\,s & 16.5\,s \\
\midrule
lean delta record (B) & \deltaLean & \deltaLeanTenth & \deltaLeanOne & \deltaLeanFar & \deltaLeanSlow \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At the 50\,Hz of the 802.11 arm the smallest value applies. On the low-rate arm records are
seconds apart, and using the 50\,ms figure there overstated what a frame could carry
(\Cref{sec:perframe}).

\paragraph{Where the chain link sits decides the floor.} \Cref{fig:curve} draws bytes per record
against batch size for three layouts. With a link in every record, the first format cannot fall
below its delta record however large the batch: at \curveMaxBatch{} records per frame it still
costs \bprFirstTwelve\,B each. Moving that link to the frame saves $32\,(1-1/b)$\,B per
record, \linkMoveSave\,B at $b = 4$, and gives \bprFirstLinkFrame\,B --- less than a
per-record link reaches at any batch drawn. The lean format's shorter header and record coding
account for the remaining \leanHeaderSave\,B, to \bprLeanDesign\,B. The two first-format
curves are sums of measured parts; the lean curve is emitted frames.

\paragraph{The generator's delta is the floor of the format.} Every one of 29\,970 delta records
at 50\,ms spacing is exactly \deltaLean\,B: nine fields, one byte each. A reviewer called that out
as looking synthetic, and it is. To find out whether it matters, the same encoder was run over
twelve public PX4 flight logs --- four quadrotors, two hexa- or octorotors, three fixed wings and
three VTOLs, \pxRecords{} in-flight records --- chosen by a rule committed before any log was
opened.

At matched spacing the generator is within a quarter of a byte of real telemetry. The spread is
by speed: the six multicopters, mostly hovering, average \pxDeltaSlow\,B per delta, and the three
fixed wings up to \pxDeltaFast\,B, for which the saving is \pxSaveWorst\%. So the mean flatters a
fast swarm by about a percentage point, and the 50\,Hz spacing of the adopted operating point is
not covered by any public log found.

\begin{remark}[What was predicted, and what was not testable]
The prediction committed in advance was a mean delta of at most $13$\,B and a saving of at least
$68\%$, \emph{at 0.1\,s spacing}. The logs turned out to carry position at 5\,Hz, so the
prediction was applied, unchanged in its numbers, at 0.2\,s --- the stricter reading, since a
difference over a longer interval is never smaller --- and holds there. The 0.1\,s prediction
itself is recorded as untested. One predicted range was missed: the keyframe, predicted at
22--25\,B, measures \pxKey\,B, because multicopters fly slowly enough for one-byte velocities.
\end{remark}
