\section{E5 --- the end-to-end configuration, and a correction to it}

Exhaustive search over encoding, scheme, placement and batch returns delta coding, Ed25519, one
signature per frame, $b = 4$. As first reported, that configuration cost $72.0$\,B per record
against $174.3$\,B for the compression-only baseline: a $58.7\%$ reduction.

\begin{remark}[That figure belonged to a design that did not meet its own constraint]
The $72.0$\,B frame amortised one keyframe over four frames. By \Cref{thm:t3prime} such a design
verifies $\lossThFour$ of its records at $p = 0.05$, not $0.95$; measured with the decoder in the
loop, \lossVFour{} (\Cref{sec:e7}). The search had admitted it because the verifiability model it
consulted assumed every frame decodes alone. With that assumption replaced by the theorem, the
search returns the same placement and batch with a keyframe in every frame.
\end{remark}

The cut in authentication bytes alone is $75.0\%$, and it is tempting to lead with it. It must not
be led with, because it reduces algebraically to $1 - 1/b$: invariant to the header, the signature
size, the encoding and the scheme. It is the definition of batching, not a result.
\end{remark}

\section{The factorial ablation, which narrows the claim}

A full factorial ablation over the four axes asks whether ``co-design'' is doing work that
single-axis optimisation would not.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Placement and batching couple exactly}, by $\ga(1 - 1/b)$ --- hence \emph{exactly
      zero} at $b = 1$, where placements A and B are byte-identical on a single-record frame.
\item \textbf{Encoding is separable in the first format.} Every interaction term is exactly zero,
      because $s$ enters additively and therefore \emph{cannot} interact.
\item \textbf{The scheme axis is byte-degenerate} among the $64$\,B schemes: they differ only in
      CPU cost.
\end{itemize}

An earlier claim that a smaller payload increases the value of batching was a \textbf{ratio-scale
artifact}: the absolute saving is $81.0$\,B for every encoding. The four-axis co-design claim was
overstated and is now precise.

\begin{remark}[One coupling the first-format ablation could not see]
In the lean format encoding and batching \emph{do} interact, in one specific way: a record can be
coded as a delta only if the record before it is in the same frame or is known to have arrived.
Row~6 of \Cref{tab:ladder} is smaller than four copies of row~5 partly for that reason. The
separability result above is a property of the first format, where each record is encoded alone.
\end{remark}

\section{E4 --- scheme selection, and the receiver's CPU}

On ARM --- the deployment platform --- Ed25519 verification is $33.2\times$ cheaper than a BLS
pairing, which keeps Ed25519 optimal for self-batching at any plausible radio/CPU power ratio. The
ordering between Ed25519 and ECDSA-P256 \emph{flips} between x86 and ARM, so the choice is
platform-specific; the byte result is identical either way, since both are $64$\,B.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{e4_crossover.png}
\caption{The scheme crossover. BLS becomes worthwhile only in the cross-signer regime.}
\label{fig:e4}
\end{figure}

A receiver verifies every neighbour's frames, and that is a second ceiling on the neighbourhood.
With $k$ signatures per frame, verification time $t_v$ and one hash per record $t_h$, one
neighbour costs $(\Lambda_i/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda_i\,t_h$ seconds of CPU per second, and one core
serves
\[
N_{\mathrm{cpu}} = 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\Lambda_i/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda_i\,t_h} \right\rfloor
\]
nodes. On the Raspberry~Pi~4 that is \ncpuBatch{} neighbours when each frame carries one Ed25519
signature and \ncpuInline{} when every record is signed. At its $\Nmax$ the design uses
\cpuLeanDesign\% of a core, so the channel binds first. Row~6 of \Cref{tab:ladder} shows the
constraint is not idle: with a signature on every record it reaches the channel's limit at
\cpuLeanInline\% of a core. With BLS a verification takes
\tVerBls\,ms and one core serves \ncpuBls{} neighbours: BLS is excluded by the CPU long before
the channel is reached, in addition to its larger signature.

Every one of these schemes spreads an authenticator across packets and still sends a frame per
record. A frame costs \fixedCost\,\si{\micro\second} of channel time before its first payload byte, so the
weight of the authenticator hardly matters: replacing a $64$\,B signature by a $13$\,B tag ---
and giving up non-repudiation --- moves $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$ from \strNsatSig{} to \strNsatMav. Four
records in a frame move it to \strNsatDesign. The stream-signing literature amortises the
\emph{cost of signing}; the cost that binds on a contended channel is the \emph{frame}.

Three things the table does not show. Tree chaining is the one classical scheme that keeps both
properties a ledger needs --- every packet verifies alone and its origin can be shown to a
third party --- and it pays
\strAuthWl\,B per packet for them. Batching has a cost no per-packet scheme has: a lost frame
takes $b$ records with it, and records wait for the batch to fill. And a scheme with a shorter
frame is lost less often at a given bit error rate (\Cref{sec:e7}), which this table does not
credit.

\section{Capacity, by direct search}\label{sec:capacity}

Bytes matter because they are airtime, and airtime decides how many neighbours can be heard. The
$\Nmax$ column of \Cref{tab:ladder} is the answer to the question an operator asks --- \emph{how
many UAVs, at what telemetry rate?} --- and it is found by simulating each configuration at its
own frame size and frame rate over a grid of neighbourhood sizes, thirty seeds per point, and
reading off where mean delivery leaves $0.95$. The interval is the 2.5--97.5 percentile of that
crossing over ten thousand bootstrap resamples of the seeds.

\begin{remark}[Do not round this to one number]
The advantage of the design is not one number. It hardly moves with the operating point ---
\ratioLean$\times$ and \ratioRelLean$\times$ in the lean format, \ratioFirst$\times$ and
\ratioRelFirst$\times$ in the first --- and it depends on the format, because the first format's
records each carry a chain link that no batch amortises. Quote it with its format. An earlier
draft said ``$\approx 3\times$ holds across readings''; the four ratios it rested on came from
the single load ceiling, ran from $1.9$ to $3.2$, and are superseded with it.
\end{remark}

The larger step is sending a quarter as many frames. Row~6 of \Cref{tab:ladder}, which shares
the frame and keeps a signature per record, already reaches \nmaxLeanInline{} neighbours
(\stepShareLean$\times$ the baseline); sharing the signature as well adds
\stepSignLean$\times$.
Every frame pays a preamble, an inter-frame space, a backoff and MAC framing whatever its length
--- about \fixedCost\,\si{\micro\second}, \fixedSharePct\% of what a baseline frame costs the
channel --- so removing three frames in four is worth more than their bytes.

\subsection{A rule for the crossing, and the test it was put to}\label{sec:line}

The envelope this chapter used to report applied one utilisation ceiling to every configuration
(\Cref{sec:envelope-history}), and \Cref{ch:validation} records how that failed. Looking at the
six crossings it failed on suggested something simpler: at the $5\%$ level, loss is a line in
airtime. With $f = \Lambda_i/b$ the frames per second a node sends and $T$ the time one frame
holds the medium (its airtime plus DIFS),
\begin{equation}
0.05 = N f\,(a\,T + c)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\Nmax \approx \frac{0.05}{f\,(a\,T + c)} .
\label{eq:line}
\end{equation}
$N f T$ is the fraction of time the neighbourhood's frames occupy the medium, so the first term
says loss grows in proportion to occupancy; the second, with $c = 8$\,\si{\micro\second}
fixed at twice the simulator's preamble-detection time, is the chance that another frame starts
inside the window in which a station cannot yet hear it. Only $a$ is fitted.

A line found by looking at six points is a hypothesis. It was treated as one: the seven
configurations that had not been simulated were named, the line's value for each was written
down with a tolerance of $\pm 6\%$, and both were committed before any of the seven was run.
The slope is $a = \lineA$, the mean of the six per-configuration values, which reproduces those
six within \lineFitWorst\%.

\textbf{The prediction held.} All seven crossings lie within \lineWorst\% of the line, and in
each of the five configurations where the two rules disagree by more than a tenth the simulation
is closer to the line than to the ceiling, which misses the thirteen simulated configurations by
up to \lineCeilWorst\%.

\begin{remark}[What this licenses, and what it does not]
\Cref{eq:line} is an empirical fit, not a model. It holds at one loss level --- loss is convex in
load, so the line says nothing about a $1\%$ or a $10\%$ target --- at one PHY rate, in one
collision domain, for frames of $146$--$565$\,B, $5$--$50$ frames per second and
\lineNlo--\lineNhi{} nodes, and in a simulator. Every capacity quoted in this thesis is a
simulated value; the line is what one would use to interpolate between them, and to see why a
ceiling on \emph{utilisation} could not work: it divides the offered load by the saturation
throughput of $N$ stations, and loss in a lightly loaded network has no reason to follow that
yardstick.
\end{remark}


\subsection{How the traffic source was chosen}

Each simulated node sends one frame per period at an instant drawn uniformly within the period.
That choice was not the first one, and how it was reached is part of the result.

The scenario this project had used throughout sends frames \emph{strictly} periodically: each
node gets one random start offset and keeps it. In such a run the relative phase of any two
nodes is frozen. Per-node output shows what that does: in the worst of thirty runs at
$N = \phaseNodes$, \phaseSilentNodes{} nodes delivered \emph{none} of their frames while
\phaseCleanNodes{} lost none at all. The simulator raises
carrier sense only at the end of a $4$\,\si{\micro\second} preamble-detection period, so two nodes
whose phases fall within that window collide in every period of the run; and nodes whose phases
put them in the same busy period contend with each other, and only each other, for all of it. A
strictly periodic run is one draw from a lottery of phase configurations, and the spread across
runs describes the lottery.

Three tests, each with its prediction committed before its runs, established what follows from
that. Jittering each frame's send time, or letting every pair of nodes drift through all relative
phases, changes the thirty-run \emph{mean} by less than the $0.005$ set in advance --- none of
sixteen comparisons exceeds $2.5$ standard errors --- while the spread between runs falls by a
factor of ten to twenty when phases are redrawn every period. The runs reported here redraw the
phase every period. Two of the predictions made along the way failed, and both failures are
recorded with the data: a jitter of $1$\,ms was predicted to halve the spread and did so at two
test points of four, and a phase sweep was predicted to bring it under $0.004$ and reached
$0.003$--$0.006$.

\begin{remark}[``Within tolerance'' is not ``zero'']
When all six cells had been run under both sources, the mean delivered fraction was higher with
redrawn phases by \srcMeanDiff{} on average over \srcPoints{} points, and every one of the six
crossings was higher, by \srcShiftLo--\srcShiftHi\%. The points are close to independent ---
the simulator hands out random streams in creation order, so runs at different node counts draw
different start offsets, and their per-seed results are uncorrelated --- which puts the average
at about two standard errors: weak evidence of a real difference of that size, and no more. If
phases are uniform and a period's losses depend only on that period's phases, the expected
difference is zero, so a real one would need a mechanism not yet identified. No reported capacity
depends on the answer, since all of them use the redrawn source; what depends on it is how the
strictly periodic figures of earlier work should be read.
\end{remark}

An earlier experiment had tested half of the assumption behind that ceiling --- that it does not
depend on frame size --- at $N = 50$ (\Cref{ch:validation}). The other half, that it does not
depend on $N$, was predicted to hold within $\pm 10\%$ in six configurations, and the prediction
was committed before the runs. \textbf{It failed.} The lean design crosses near $118$--$124$
neighbours where the ceiling says $142$; the first format's shared-frame configuration crosses
\emph{above} the ceiling's value. The signs differ between configurations, so the ceiling cannot
be re-tuned. The reason is not subtle in hindsight: $U$ divides the offered load by the
saturation throughput of $N$ stations, which falls as $N$ grows, while the loss of a lightly
loaded network depends on how much airtime the traffic occupies and not on how many stations
produce it.

The ratios of the old envelope had been called ``protected by construction'', because numerator
and denominator used the same ceiling. They were protected against an error in the ceiling's
\emph{value}. They were not protected against the ceiling being the wrong \emph{kind} of
quantity, and they moved.

\section{Certificates}

The rows so far assume that receivers already hold the senders' public keys. Vehicular practice
sends a full certificate in every fifth message and an eight-octet digest in the
others~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}. Charging that to every frame --- a \certBytes\,B certificate, hence
\certPerFrame\,B per frame on average --- gives the ``with cert.'' column of \Cref{tab:ladder}.
Certificates are a per-frame cost, so batching amortises them too, and both the baseline and the
design are charged.

\begin{remark}[Why the order of this work matters]
The certificate term was added to the byte model \emph{before} the published figures it is
compared with in \Cref{ch:background} were retrieved. Adding it afterwards would have been
fitting the correction to the answer.
\end{remark}
