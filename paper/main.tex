\abstract{Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) that broadcast signed, hash-chained telemetry spend
more bytes on authentication than on data: a 64\,B signature and a 32\,B chain link accompany a
\keyLean\,B record. AUTHBC is a frame format, sender and receiver in which one signature and one
chain link cover $b$ records, yet every frame verifies alone. Batch size, encoding and signature
scheme are selected jointly under five constraints: freshness, verifiability, channel capacity,
receiver CPU and payload size. Measured frames, ns-3 simulation and metered sender energy give
two feasibility boundaries. Where the frame fits (50~records/s, 100\,ms deadline), AUTHBC sends
\bprLeanDesign\,B per record against \bprLeanBase\,B for per-record signing, and one simulated
802.11a collision domain carries \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes instead of \nmaxLeanBase{} at 5\% frame
loss: the cost that binds is the frame, not the authenticator. Batching gains nothing unless
record rate times deadline exceeds two. Where it does not fit, \exclCount{} of the twelve
EU863-870 LoRaWAN data rates cannot carry it. Capacities are simulated; when the swarm spreads,
fewer neighbors are in reach, but the gain stays \spRatioLo--\mvRatioHi-fold, moving or not. On
hardware, contention was measured between two radios only.}

\keywords{FANET, UAV telemetry, broadcast authentication, digital signatures, hash chain,
batching, frame aggregation, age of information, formal verification, IEEE 802.11 DCF, LoRaWAN,
ns-3}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Flying Ad-hoc Networks (FANETs) let swarms of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) coordinate without
central infrastructure. Each vehicle reports its position, velocity and status to its neighbors
many times per second. When these reports must later serve as evidence --- of where each vehicle
was, and of what it claimed --- every report must be attributable to its sender, and the
sequence must be tamper-evident. The standard construction signs every record and links it to
its predecessor by a hash. Once the payload is compact, however, the protection becomes larger
than the data it protects. Our earlier comparison of encoding formats showed that canonical
Concise Binary Object Representation (CBOR) reduces an 18-field telemetry message from 358\,B in
JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) to 130\,B~\cite{farouk2026encoding}. The compactly encoded
position, velocity and status record of this work occupies about \keyLean\,B, whereas an Ed25519
or Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) signature occupies 64\,B and a SHA-256
chain link 32\,B. On a shared radio channel these bytes become airtime, and airtime determines
how many neighbors can be heard.

Current practice authenticates each message individually. MAVLink~2 appends a 13\,B symmetric
tag to each message~\cite{mavlink2signing}, and vehicular standards attach an ECDSA signature,
and periodically a certificate, to each one~\cite{etsi_ts103097}. Existing stream-signing
schemes~\cite{gennaro1997streams,wong1999flows,perrig2000emss} amortize the cost of signing
across packets without reducing the number of frames that a sender places on the channel.
Conversely, aggregate-signature schemes~\cite{li2025clas} reduce the work of the verifier
without reducing what each signer transmits. Letting several records share one frame, one
signature and one chain link is an established alternative, and its price is known in kind:
records wait for the frame to fill, and a lost frame loses all of
them~\cite{skordoulis2008aggregation}. What prior work does not provide is the accounting: what a frame must carry so that a receiver can verify it without any other
frame, on which links such a frame does not fit at any batch size, which constraint caps the
batch where it fits, and what the saved bytes buy on a contended channel in nodes and not in
percent. This gap leaves system designers without data-driven guidance for selecting batch
size, encoding and signature scheme jointly.

This paper presents AUTHBC (AUTHenticated telemetry for BlockChain-style ledgers) as a frame
format, a sender and a receiver. The system was implemented, measured and searched over its
parameters. Its central observation is that on a contended broadcast channel the cost that binds
is the frame, not the authenticator. The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Byte and capacity gain.} One signature and one chain link per four records cut the
bytes per record by \saveLean\% and raise the simulated capacity of one 802.11a collision domain
from \nmaxLeanBase{} to \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes. A step-by-step comparison shows that most of the
gain comes from sending fewer frames (Sections~\ref{sec:bytes} and~\ref{sec:capacity}).
\item \textbf{Capacity law and mechanism.} An airtime capacity law with one fitted constant
reproduces the simulated capacities. A contention model with nothing fitted explains the
constant and predicted cases that had not been simulated, other batch sizes and loss levels
among them (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{Binding constraints.} Freshness, not the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU), caps the
batch at four records. A frame that depends on its predecessor misses the verifiability target,
so every frame carries a key record. A Rust receiver serves \rxRustNodes{} nodes per core, so the
channel, not the processor, binds (Sections~\ref{sec:loss}--\ref{sec:cpu}).
\item \textbf{Exclusion bound.} \exclCountCap{} of the twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN data rates cannot
carry one signed, chained frame: \exclSigCount{} because the signature alone does not fit, and
\exclFmtCount{} because of the header of this format. The five conditions of the bound, and what
each relaxation buys, are stated (Section~\ref{sec:lowrate}).
\item \textbf{Security model and defenses.} Origin, integrity, replay protection, equivocation
evidence, gap detection and continuity are proven in the Tamarin prover, in three separate
models. Two defenses are measured: a timestamp acceptance window against delayed frames, and an
8\,B group message authentication code (MAC) against floods from outside the swarm
(Section~\ref{sec:security}).
\end{itemize}

Batching several records under one signature is not new. What this paper adds is the
accounting --- which links can carry a self-verifying frame, which constraint caps the batch, and
what the saved bytes buy in nodes on a contended channel --- together with a measured
implementation. AUTHBC introduces no new cryptographic primitive. Each result states whether it
is measured, simulated or only modeled; Section~\ref{sec:limits} collects the limitations, and
Table~\ref{tab:decision} summarizes which configuration to use.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Our work intersects per-message authentication, stream signing, aggregate and batch
verification, hash-linked records, compact encoding for small payloads, and broadcast channel
models.

\runin{Per-Message Authentication} MAVLink~2 signing appends a link identifier, a 48-bit
timestamp and a 48-bit truncated SHA-256 tag computed with a shared
secret~\cite{mavlink2signing}. This mechanism authenticates a \emph{link}: any holder of the key
can produce any message, so a record cannot later be attributed to one vehicle. Rotta and
Mykytyn likewise attach a keyed hash (HMAC) and a timestamp to each broadcast telemetry message
of a swarm~\cite{rotta2024telemetrybroadcast}. Vehicular broadcast uses public-key signatures:
ECDSA over each message, with the signer's certificate in some messages and an eight-octet
digest of it in the others~\cite{etsi_ts103097,ndss2024pqv2v}. We adopt this trust model, and
its per-message cost is the baseline of our evaluation.

\runin{Stream Signing} To amortize one signature over many packets, Gennaro and Rohatgi sign
the first packet and chain the others by hashes~\cite{gennaro1997streams}; one lost packet
breaks the chain. Wong and Lam sign a hash tree over a block and send in every packet the hashes
needed to reach the root~\cite{wong1999flows}. Each packet then verifies alone, and the signing
cost is amortized but the bytes are not. EMSS tolerates loss by carrying several hashes per
packet and a periodic signature packet. TESLA replaces the signature by a MAC whose key is
disclosed later~\cite{perrig2000emss,perrig2002tesla}, and TBRD applies
TESLA to UAS Remote ID with about half the overhead of signatures~\cite{veara2025tbrd}. Erasure
codes spread the hashes and the signature of a block over its packets, so that the block
verifies when enough of them arrive, for 10\,B per packet in blocks of sixteen at 5\% loss and a
latency of one block~\cite{pannetrat2003multicast}. All these schemes spread \emph{one
authenticator across packets}, so packets depend on each other or on a later one. AUTHBC
places \emph{several records in one packet}, which a small telemetry record allows. Each frame
then stays self-verifying, and the cost moves to latency at the sender. TESLA instead delays
verification at the receiver, and a disclosed MAC key gives no non-repudiation, which a ledger
requires. Section~\ref{sec:clas} places these schemes at our operating point.

\runin{Aggregate and Batch Verification} BLS signatures aggregate~\cite{bls2001,bgls2003}, and
certificateless aggregate signature (CLAS) schemes for vehicular networks build on such
constructions~\cite{li2025clas}. Zhang \emph{et al.} verify many signatures at once at a roadside
unit~\cite{zhang2008batch}. Rajasekaran \emph{et al.} batch-authenticate a group of drones with a
pairing-based scheme and report a communication cost that grows linearly with the
group~\cite{rajasekaran2022batchfanet}. All of these reduce the \emph{work} of the verifier.
Each message, however, still carries its own signature material as its signer sends it
(Section~\ref{sec:clas}).

\runin{Hash-Linked Records} Linking each record to the hash of its predecessor to make a
sequence tamper-evident is due to Haber and Stornetta~\cite{haber1991timestamp}; blockchain
applications on UAVs are surveyed in~\cite{mehta2020blockchainuav}. We take the chain as given
and quantify what it costs on air.

\runin{Compact Encoding and Small Payloads} That a signature leaves little room in a small frame
is established. G{\"u}ndo{\u{g}}an \emph{et al.} find 9\,B of application data left beside an
Ed25519 signature in an IEEE 802.15.4 frame~\cite{gundogan2021firmware}. Post-quantum signatures
do not fit one 5G broadcast block~\cite{arxiv2510pqsib1}. For Remote~ID, a 64-octet signature is
paged over 25-octet Bluetooth frames with a parity page~\cite{rfc9575}. This is a form of
spreading and recovery that our scope excludes. Automatic Dependent Surveillance--Broadcast
(ADS-B) is the extreme case: its 112-bit messages leave 56 bits for data. Proposals for
public-key authentication there distribute one signature over several messages that receivers
buffer~\cite{strohmeier2015adsb}. Compact encodings~\cite{rfc8949} and delta coding of time
series~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} are established, and our earlier evaluation found canonical
CBOR byte-identical across 10,000 trials~\cite{farouk2026encoding}. The AUTHBC frame is a
canonical CBOR map.

\runin{Channel Models} Broadcast frames are not acknowledged, and their contention window never
doubles. The saturation model of Bianchi for the IEEE 802.11 Distributed Coordination Function
(DCF)~\cite{bianchi2000} therefore does not apply as it stands; we use the broadcast model of Ma
and Chen~\cite{machen2007,machen2008} and validate it against ns-3~\cite{nsnam}. For LoRa,
Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.} model single-gateway scalability~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora}, and Zirak
\emph{et al.} report air-to-air delivery against range on hardware~\cite{zirak2021swarm}. These
models describe the channel alone. Our evaluation applies them to each authentication
configuration at its own frame size and frame rate.

Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation, and Fig.~\ref{fig:system} gives an overview. Each
UAV codes, chains and signs its records into frames and broadcasts them on a shared channel.
Each receiver decodes, verifies and stores the frames of its neighbors. Five constraints fix the
design parameters (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}).

\runin{Network and Traffic} $N$ UAVs share one channel and all hear one another: one collision
domain, which we call a neighborhood of $N$ nodes. Each UAV produces $\Lambda$ telemetry records
per second and broadcasts them, without acknowledgments or retransmissions. The primary
operating point is $\Lambda = 50$~records/s with a freshness bound $D_{\max} = 100$\,ms. This rate
is the companion-computer stream rate of PX4~\cite{px4_mavlink_streams}.
The pair lies within the 3GPP requirements for direct UAV-to-UAV broadcast: at least
10 messages per second and an end-to-end latency of at most 100\,ms (TS~22.125, R-5.2.2-010 and
R-5.2.2-011~\cite{3gpp_ts22125}).
A relaxed point of 20~records/s and 250\,ms is reported for comparison.

\runin{Telemetry Record} Each record carries nine fields, all fixed-point integers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{ts} -- timestamp in milliseconds
\item \texttt{lat}, \texttt{lon} -- latitude and longitude in degrees$\times 10^{7}$
\item \texttt{alt} -- altitude in centimeters
\item \texttt{vel\_x}, \texttt{vel\_y}, \texttt{vel\_z} -- velocity components in cm/s
\item \texttt{battery} -- battery level in percent
\item \texttt{mode} -- flight mode (enumeration)
\end{itemize}
In the ledger, each record also carries the identifier of its sender, its sequence number and
the hash of its predecessor.

\runin{Ledger} Each UAV keeps an append-only chain of its own records,
$\mathit{prev\_hash}_{i} = \mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{rec}_{i-1})$, and stores the verified
records it hears from others. No consensus layer is included; the subject of this paper is
authenticated dissemination. A ledger can be audited from the uploaded frames alone, with the
public keys of the senders (Section~\ref{sec:security}).

\runin{Adversary} The adversary controls the radio: it reads, injects, replays, modifies, delays
and drops frames, and may operate many radios. It holds no private key of an honest UAV.

\runin{What Is Protected} (i)~\emph{Origin and integrity:} a stored record was produced by the
UAV it names and was not altered; a public-key signature is checked before anything is stored.
(ii)~\emph{Replay:} sequence numbers strictly increase per sender, so a repeated frame is
refused. (iii)~\emph{Equivocation:} two validly signed records for one sequence number are
detected, and the two frames that carried them are kept. The signature covers a frame, so a
record is evidence only with its frame; a third party checks the pair with the sender's public
key alone. Since only a holder of the sender's key can sign either record, an equivocation
identifies a misbehaving or compromised UAV. (iv)~\emph{Continuity:} omission or reordering
within what a receiver holds is detected through the chain. (v)~\emph{Bounded-delay freshness:}
while GNSS time is available, a receiver refuses a frame whose timestamp is more than $\Delta$
older than its own clock (Section~\ref{sec:delay}).

\runin{What Is Not Protected} Telemetry is sent in clear. Jamming is not resisted: loss appears
as the robustness measure $V$ below, not as a security property. Flooding is resisted only from
outside the swarm. An 8\,B group MAC, under a key that all members hold, refuses an outsider's
frame before it is decoded. A member holding that key can still make a receiver verify forged
frames (Section~\ref{sec:flooding}). A frame delayed by less than $\Delta$ is accepted, authentic
and in order, and so is a later one once GNSS time has been lost for longer than the window
allows. A UAV with a valid key can sign false data; only inconsistent falsehood is detectable.
Key distribution, revocation and admission are assumed: receivers hold the public keys of the
senders, and the bytes of delivering certificates are charged separately
(Section~\ref{sec:certs}). Senders are not anonymous.

\runin{Formal Analysis} Properties (i)--(iv) and the detection of gaps are proven in the Tamarin
prover~\cite{tamarin} for unboundedly many frames, and (v) is shown to hold with the window and
to fail without it. The proofs use three separate models, because one model with both the chain
and the clock did not terminate. The three models are not composed. That the properties hold
together for the full protocol is argued, not proven: the receiver of the model without chain
and clock checks less than the full receiver, so it accepts a superset of what the full receiver
accepts. We also assume canonical encoding to be injective: two different sets of records never
encode to the same bytes.

\runin{Verifiability Metric} $V$ is the probability that a record a UAV generates is received,
decoded and verified by a given neighbor. We require $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ and take
$\epsilon = 0.05$ throughout.

\runin{Frame Structure} A frame is one canonical CBOR map~\cite{rfc8949} with integer keys
(Table~\ref{tab:fields}, Fig.~\ref{fig:system}); Appendix~\ref{app:frame} gives the complete
layout. The fields \texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq} name the sender and the sequence number of
the first record. The field \texttt{link} is the hash of the record before it, \texttt{recs} is
the record stream, and \texttt{auth} is one signature. A record in the stream consists of nine
variable-length integers. The first record of a frame is a \emph{key record}, carrying absolute
values (\keyLean\,B on average). Each later one is a \emph{delta record}, the difference from the
record before it (\deltaLean\,B, the floor of the format, at the 20 and 50\,ms spacings of our two
operating points). The sender identifier and sequence number are not repeated in the records. A
frame that can be decoded and verified without any other frame is \emph{self-verifying}; every
AUTHBC frame is.

\runin{Signed Content and Verification} The signature covers not the record stream but the
canonical ledger records, each with its own \texttt{prev\_hash}. The receiver first rebuilds
them: the first \texttt{prev\_hash} is \texttt{link}, and each later one is the hash of the
record just rebuilt. Only then does it check the signature. A frame that cannot be decoded
therefore cannot be verified, and nothing unverified is stored. Each verified record is offered
to the ledger store. The store refuses a sequence number that is not newer, keeps an
equivocating pair as evidence, and flags a link that contradicts a record it holds. The decoding
state advances only after a frame has been verified and stored, so a replayed frame cannot
desynchronize a stream. Flipping any single bit of a frame makes verification fail; all
\bitflips{} positions of a four-record frame were tried.

\runin{Behavior Under Frame Loss} When a frame is lost, the next one still decodes and verifies,
because it starts with a key record. Its \texttt{link} then names a record that this receiver
does not hold. The gap shows in the sequence numbers, and the link is stored unchecked, to be
checked by any party that holds the missing record. A late copy of the lost frame is refused,
since sequence numbers must increase; the gap is not repaired.

\runin{One Chain Link per Frame} The ledger still chains every record, but only the first link of
a frame travels; the receiver recomputes the others. Inside a frame, the order and content of
records therefore rest on the frame signature alone. In the record-linked format below, every
record also carries its own hash on air. A frame is signed and accepted as a unit, so those
hashes are redundant and a receiver loses no check. However, this leaves one protection
mechanism inside a frame instead of two.

\runin{The Record-Linked Format} For comparison we keep the format we built first, the
\emph{record-linked format}: text keys, and in every record its own chain link, sender
identifier and sequence number. The format described above, with integer keys and one chain
link per frame, is the \emph{frame-linked format}; it is \leanVsFirstPct\% smaller. The
record-linked header is \hdrFirst\,B, of which \keyNamesFirst\,B are key names. That format is
implemented but no longer developed. Its sizes in this paper are estimated from separately
measured components, and its frames with delta records were never produced; such sizes are
marked wherever they appear.

\runin{Design Parameters} A configuration is defined by four parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Batch size} $b$ -- the number of records in a frame.
\item \emph{Signature placement} -- a signature covers each record (\emph{per-record signing};
\texttt{auth} is then an array of $b$ signatures) or the whole frame (\emph{per-frame signing}).
\item \emph{Signature scheme} -- Ed25519~\cite{ed25519} and ECDSA P-256~\cite{fips186} at 64\,B,
or BLS12-381~\cite{bls2001} at 96\,B in the library used.
\item \emph{Key-record interval} $r$ -- one frame in $r$ starts with a key record, and the
others start with a delta record against the previous frame.
\end{itemize}
Throughout, \emph{AUTHBC} denotes the frame-linked format with $b=4$, one signature per frame,
delta records and $r=1$. \emph{Per-record signing} with one record per frame ($b=1$) is the
baseline.

\subsection{Constraints}\label{sec:constraints}
Five constraints decide which configurations are feasible: bytes, freshness, verifiability,
channel capacity and receiver CPU select the configuration, and payload decides where a frame
can be sent at all.

\runin{Bytes} With header $H_f$, link field $L$, signature \ba, key record $s_k$ and delta record
$s_\delta$, a self-verifying frame of $b$ records with one signature costs, per record,
\begin{equation}
B(b) = \frac{H_f + L + \ba + s_k + (b-1)\,s_\delta}{b}.
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
$B(1)$ is per-record signing: one record, one signature, one frame. Signing each record of a
shared frame replaces \ba{} by $b\,\ba$. Equation~\eqref{eq:bytes} is exact to within a byte of
CBOR length prefix; for the frame-linked format we report the lengths of frames produced by the
implementation.

\runin{Freshness} The oldest record in a frame waits for the frame to fill. Adding the
channel-access and airtime delay $T_{ch}$ and the verification time $t_v$,
\begin{equation}
D(b) = \frac{b}{\Lambda} + T_{ch} + t_v \;\leq\; D_{\max}.
\label{eq:fresh}
\end{equation}
Fill time dominates. At both operating points $\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$: five records fill exactly
$D_{\max}$ and leave nothing for the channel, so $b \leq 4$ whatever the encoding or the
scheme.\footnote{Equation~\eqref{eq:fresh} counts the whole filling period $b/\Lambda$, since a
record may describe a state up to one sampling interval old. With $b/\Lambda$, the bound is on
the \emph{peak age} of information~\cite{yates2021aoi} at a receiver that loses no frame: the
delay of the newest record of a frame plus the interval to the next frame. Counting only the
wait $(b-1)/\Lambda$ would admit $b=5$ and \bprLeanFive\,B per record.} The 1500\,B MTU would
admit \mtuRecsFirst{} records in the record-linked format and \mtuRecsLean{} in the frame-linked
one. Because the whole sampling interval is counted, even one record misses the deadline when
$\Lambda D_{\max} \leq 1$.

\runin{Verifiability} If one frame in $r$ is self-verifying, frame $j$ of a group verifies only if
it and the $j-1$ frames before it arrive. With independent frame loss $p$,
\begin{equation}
V(r,p) = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-p)^{j},
\label{eq:v}
\end{equation}
which equals $1-p$ at $r=1$ and is smaller for every $r>1$. A target $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon \leq p$ therefore admits $r=1$ only. The same argument forbids splitting a signed unit
over $n$ frames, which then verifies with probability $(1-p)^n$.

\runin{Channel} For a configuration --- a frame size and a frame rate per node --- the capacity
$N_{\max}$ is the largest neighborhood in which the mean delivered fraction of frames is at least
$1-\epsilon$. It is obtained by simulation (Section~\ref{sec:sim}). As a conservative companion
we report $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$, the largest $N$ whose offered frame rate does not exceed the
saturation throughput of the broadcast DCF model~\cite{machen2008}.

\runin{Receiver CPU} A receiver verifies the frames of every neighbor. With $k$ signatures per
frame, verification time $t_v$ and hash time $t_h$, one neighbor costs
$(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h$ seconds of CPU per second, and one core serves
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{cpu}} = 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:cpu}
\end{equation}
nodes, itself included.

\runin{Payload} A self-verifying frame must fit the maximum payload $M$ of the link:
\begin{equation}
M \;\geq\; H_f + L + \ba + s_k .
\label{eq:excl}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Telemetry workload}\label{sec:workload}
Records come from a seeded random-walk generator: \seeds{} seeds of 1000 records at 50\,ms
spacing, that of the relaxed point. The delta record sits at the floor of the format,
\deltaLean\,B, at both spacings (Section~\ref{sec:px4}). Header fields are sized for a five-digit
sender identifier one hour into a 50\,Hz flight. Section~\ref{sec:px4} checks the generator
against flight logs.

\subsection{Timing and energy measurement}\label{sec:measure}
\runin{Timing} Signing, verification, hashing and encoding are timed on the Raspberry~Pi~4 and
reported as medians: Ed25519 sign/verify \tSignEd/\tVerEd\us, ECDSA \tSignEc/\tVerEc\us, BLS
\tSignBls/\tVerBls\,ms, and SHA-256 \tHash\us.

\runin{Energy} Sender energy is metered with an INA219 sensor on the supply rail, with five
repetitions per configuration and idle power subtracted. The record-linked format was metered on
one board; the frame-linked format, in Python and in Rust, on a second board and sensor of the
same types.

\subsection{Channel simulation}\label{sec:sim}
The channel is simulated in ns-3.48~\cite{nsnam}: IEEE~802.11a at 6\,Mb/s, ad hoc mode and
broadcast frames, with all nodes in range at equal received power, so there is no capture and no
hidden terminal. Each node sends one frame every $b/\Lambda$ seconds, at an instant drawn
uniformly within each period, so that the relative phases of the nodes change from frame to
frame. A strictly periodic source --- one that sends each frame as soon as it fills, as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fresh} assumes --- freezes every phase for a whole run: a run then samples one phase
configuration, in which a few nodes may collide in every period while the others lose nothing.
The two sources deliver the same on average: over \srcPoints{} points and \srcFreshRuns{} runs,
the means differ by \srcFreshD{} $\pm$ \srcFreshSe. The strictly periodic one varies
\srcSpreadLo--\srcSpreadHi{} times more from run to run (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Capacity
depends on the frame rate and length, not on where in its period a frame is sent. Capacities are
therefore simulated with the randomized source and freshness is evaluated with send-at-fill
timing; the ages of the randomized source run half a period longer
(Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives $N_{\max}$ as the largest simulated neighborhood whose
mean delivery over \seeds{} seeds is at least 0.95, with its bootstrap interval. The figures and
Tables~\ref{tab:phy} and~\ref{tab:stream} give the crossing of 0.95, interpolated between the two
simulated sizes around it, from the same runs: \crossDesign{} \crossCiDesign{} for AUTHBC and
\crossSig{} \crossCiSig{} for per-record signing.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}
A run lasts 20\,s, and each point comprises \seeds{} runs. $N_{\max}$ is the largest simulated $N$
whose mean delivered fraction is at least 0.95, with every smaller simulated $N$ also passing. Its
interval is the 2.5--97.5 percentile of that quantity over 10\,000 bootstrap resamples of the
seeds. An interval of a mean over seeds is a Student-$t$ interval, and an interval of an
interpolated capacity is an expanded percentile bootstrap
interval~\cite{hesterberg2015bootstrap}. With a known truth and \seeds{} seeds, the plain
percentile interval covered \covPlainLo--\covPlainHi\% of the time, and these intervals
\covFixedLo--\covFixedHi\%. The saturation models were validated separately against saturated
ns-3 runs (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). Where an experiment tests a prediction, the prediction
was fixed before the experiment ran, and misses are reported as misses.

The main evaluation uses one collision domain of static nodes. Section~\ref{sec:robust} repeats
it for a swarm spread over a wider area, with hidden stations, and for a moving swarm.
Capacities are obtained by simulation, and contention was measured on hardware between two
radios only (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

the target under every one of them, intervals included. At a looser budget the margin can be too
small: at $\epsilon = \mgMissEps$ and $p = \mgMissP$, the interval falls below the target under
bursts of eight frames and under bit errors, although the mean does not.

\subsection{Why capacity is where it is}\label{sec:mechanism}

\runin{The Airtime Capacity Law} At 5\% loss, capacity follows a law in airtime. Let $f =
\Lambda/b$ be the frames per second a node sends, $T$ the time one frame holds the medium (its
airtime plus DIFS, the DCF inter-frame space), $c$ twice the preamble-detection time of the
simulator, and $a$ one fitted constant. Then
\begin{equation}
N_{\max} \approx \frac{0.05}{f\,(a\,T + c)}, \qquad a = \lineA,\quad c = 8\,\mu\mathrm{s}.
\label{eq:line}
\end{equation}
We call Eq.~\eqref{eq:line} the airtime capacity law; it is the capacity envelope of the title.
It was fitted to six rows of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8), which it then
reproduces within \lineFitWorst\%. Twelve further configurations were predicted from the law
before they were simulated. Seven of our own all lie within \lineWorst\% of it.
The other five, the one-record frames of the stream-signing schemes in
Table~\ref{tab:stream}, lie \lineStreamLeast--\lineStreamWorst\% above the law's prediction.
A single utilization ceiling, measured at one point and applied to all, misses the first thirteen
(six fitted, seven predicted) by up to \lineCeilWorst\%, in both directions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:line} is a fit at one loss level and one PHY rate, checked for frames of
\lineFrameLo--\lineFrameHi\,B, \lineFpsLo--\lineFpsHi~frames/s and \lineNlo--\lineNhi{} nodes;
the tables quote simulated values, not the law.

All of those configurations hold one record or four. Batches of two, three and eight, which
deadlines of 60, 80 and 180\,ms would admit, were predicted before they were simulated. Their
capacities are \batNsTwo, \batNsThree{} and \batNsEight{} nodes, within \batLineWorst\% of the
law and \batModelWorst\% of the contention model. Fig.~\ref{fig:deadline} places them on the law:
it shows what a deadline buys at this record rate, and at 40\,ms it buys nothing.

\includegraphics{fig_capacity_vs_deadline.png}
\caption{Nodes AUTHBC serves at 50~records/s, by batch size and the smallest deadline
that admits it: simulated capacities and the airtime capacity law. At 40\,ms the batch is one

\label{fig:deadline}

\runin{The Contention Model} The constant $a$ is not free. A frame is lost in two ways. Two
stations that found the medium busy may draw the same backoff slot, a mechanism known from
analyses of vehicular safety broadcast~\cite{ma2009dsrc,cao2021dsrc}. Or a station decides to
send within the 4\us{} in which it cannot yet sense another; that is $c$.
This is the vulnerable period of carrier sensing~\cite{kleinrock1975csma}, with the
detection time in place of the propagation delay; the DCF broadcast analyses above omit it.
We call an event simulation of the DCF access procedure with these two mechanisms, with nothing
fitted and none of the simulator's code, the \emph{contention model}. It reproduces all
\modelCells{} simulated capacities within \modelWorst\%, and it predicted all \modelPredCases{}
cases that had not been simulated --- the contention window doubled, and loss levels of 2\% and
10\% --- within \modelPredWorst\% (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}).

\runin{Models Against the Simulator} The broadcast model~\cite{machen2008} matches saturated ns-3
runs within $\pm\valBcast$\% over $N=5$--50 (\seeds{} seeds).

\runin{Contention Model Against Two Radios} On two Raspberry~Pi~4 radios, one transmitter alone
loses \hwLossFrames{} frames in \hwSentFrames{} ($p = \hwLoss$). With both radios saturated,
\hwSatOff\% of the frames put on air are lost where the contention model gives \hwSatStd\%, as
predicted before the run. Below saturation the agreement is poorer. As the medium fills from half
to 85\%, the radios lose \hwPoolLowRatio, \hwPoolMidRatio{} and \hwPoolHighRatio{} times what the
model predicts; the prediction made before the run held at the first two loads and failed at the
third. The cause is not established. Below saturation a radio often holds two frames of its own,
and a radio of this family sends its queued frames closer together than the standard allows: one
per \hwCycleOn\,ms against \hwCycleStd\,ms, as commercial cards are known
to~\cite{bianchi2007cards}. That state is common with two busy radios and rare at the operating
point. However, a follow-up that switched off the vendor's frame bursting failed all four of its
predictions: bursting has no demonstrated effect on the loss. No capacity was measured: the
capacities in this paper rest on simulation, and they are the capacities of radios that keep the
DCF access procedure.

\subsection{Receiver CPU}\label{sec:cpu}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cpu} charges cryptography only. By it, one core of the Raspberry~Pi~4 serves a
neighborhood of \ncpuBatch{} nodes when each frame carries one Ed25519 signature, and of
\ncpuInline{} when every record is signed. At its capacity AUTHBC uses \cpuLeanDesign\% of a
core, so by this count the channel binds first. Row~6 shows that the count can bind: with a
signature on every record, it reaches the limit of the channel at \cpuLeanInline\% of a core.
With BLS a verification takes \tVerBls\,ms and one core serves \ncpuBls{} nodes. On this
hardware BLS is therefore excluded by CPU long before the channel, in addition to its larger
signature. Ed25519 was designed for batch verification~\cite{ed25519}: on the Pi~4, a batch of 64
takes \edBatchRatio{} of the time per signature. The Python prototype verifies one signature at
a time, and ECDSA does not admit batches.

Decoding, not cryptography, limits the full Python receiver. A four-record frame takes
\leanRecvMs\,ms to receive on the Pi~4, \leanRecvOverVerify{} times its verification, so one core
serves \ncpuProto{} nodes and the processor binds before the channel. Run as one receiver process
per core, one to four at once, the board serves \mcNodesOne, \mcNodesTwo, \mcNodesThree{} and
\mcNodesFour{} nodes.
Four cores receive \mcScaleFour{} times the frames of one, less than we had written down
beforehand, so \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes need three of them.
In Rust, the same receive path is \rxSpeedup{} times faster, and the channel binds again
(Table~\ref{tab:receiver}). A receiver can also use records as soon as their group MAC and frame
are checked, and verify their signatures in batches before they enter the ledger. This deferred
batch verification serves \rxLazyNodes{} nodes per core. A record is then evidence within
\rxEvidenceMs\,ms by the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fresh}; within that window, a member holding
the group key could have records acted on that are later purged.
The Python receiver was timed in three sessions on the same board, at
\pyRecvLo--\pyRecvHi\,ms per frame (within \pyRecvSpreadPct\%); each table and paragraph quotes
the session it reports, and Section~\ref{sec:flooding} adds the admission layer.

\subsection{Certificates}\label{sec:certs}
The rows so far assume that receivers already hold the public keys of the senders. Vehicular
practice sends a full certificate in every fifth message and an eight-octet digest in the
others~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}. Charging that to every frame --- a \certBytes\,B certificate, so
\certPerFrame\,B per frame on average --- gives the first certificate column of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (per-record signing: \certLeanBase\,B vs.\ AUTHBC: \certLeanDesign\,B per
record). That reading favors AUTHBC. The practice it copies sends ten messages a second, so one in
five is one certificate per \certIntervalMs\,ms; per-record signing at 50 frames/s, charged per
frame, sends five times that. The second column therefore charges one certificate per
\certIntervalMs\,ms at any frame rate (per-record signing: \certTLeanBase\,B vs.\ AUTHBC:
\certTLeanDesign\,B), and batching then amortizes only the digest. These are explicit
certificates. Implicit ones are shorter (a signed message of at most 226\,B against
330\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}) and are not priced, so both columns are upper bounds.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:robust}
The results above assume one collision domain of static nodes at equal received power, one PHY
rate, Ed25519 and synthetic telemetry. This section varies each.

\runin{Unequal Received Power and Capture} All results so far place every node at one point, with
equal received power, so a frame that overlaps another is lost everywhere. With the nodes placed
uniformly in a disc and power falling as in free space, a receiver keeps the strongest of the
frames that start together if it is 4\,dB above the rest. The contention model with that one
rule, read in the source of the simulator, gave \sprModelBase{} and \sprModelDesign{} nodes for
per-record signing and AUTHBC before the runs. In a disc of radius 15\,m, where every station
still senses every other by energy, ns-3 gives \sprNearBase{} and \sprNearDesign. At 100\,m, the
scale of a swarm, it gives \sprFarBase{} and \sprFarDesign, at most \sprFarBelowPct\% lower. The
equal-power capacities of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} are therefore conservative for unequal power,
by \sprGainLo--\sprGainHi\%, and the capacity ratio stays \sprRatioNear$\times$. Both discs keep
every pair in range: hidden stations, which would lower the capacity, are not in this test.

\runin{A Spread, Moving Swarm} Hidden stations appear when a swarm is spread wider than one
sensing range. We placed the nodes in discs of radius 100\,m to 1\,km, with log-distance path
loss, Nakagami fading and the same capture rule, and also in a grouped and a moving layout.
Across the spread and moving layouts, the capacity ratio of AUTHBC to per-record signing stays
between \spRatioLo{} and \mvRatioHi{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:spread}).
At a radius of 1\,km, \spHiddenPct\% of the interferers of a frame cannot hear its
sender. Spread over that disc, the channel carries more nodes, \spArea{} with AUTHBC and
\spAreaBase{} with per-record signing, but each node has fewer neighbors within reach:
\spNeighbours{} and \spNeighboursBase, against \nmaxLeanDesign{} and \nmaxLeanBase{} nodes in one
collision domain, because a receiver contends with every node in its carrier-sensing
range~\cite{hu2020hidden}. A node's neighbors within reach are the other nodes within
\spReach\,m of it, the largest distance at which an isolated link delivers at least 99\% of
frames; they are counted for every frame sent and read at the 95\% delivery crossing.
With every node on its own Gauss--Markov path at up to 15\,m/s~\cite{bujari2017fanet}, delivery
changes by at most \mvDeltaV{}. The channel model has no Doppler and no time-correlated fading.

\runin{Choosing the Batch at Run Time}\label{sec:adaptive} A controller that sets $b$ from the

before the runs, with a margin of 0.01, it met $V \geq 0.95$ in \adRegShare\% of one-second
windows and failed at \adRegFail{} nodes. With a margin of 0.02 it met the target in \adShare\% of
windows and failed at \adFail, against \adFixShare\% and \adFixFail{} with $b=4$ fixed, while
saving \adAgeSaved\,ms of peak age (Fig.~\ref{fig:adaptive}). These ages come from the randomized
source of the capacity runs, which sends each frame at a random instant of the next period. They
are therefore on average half a frame period longer than the send-at-fill ages of
Section~\ref{sec:fresh}, and they are not comparable with $D_{\max}$. Choosing $b$ at run time
buys freshness at the cost of reliability margin; it does not raise capacity. The nearest
published mechanism adapts the contention window to a flocking swarm~\cite{huang2021fmac}.

\runin{Ledger Audit} One pass over the frames of a mission, with the public keys of the senders,
finds every equivocation and every frame that does not verify. For \auUavs{} UAVs over a flight
hour, a planted equivocation and a planted forgery were both found, with no false finding, in
\auMinutes{} minutes of one core.
The audit covers the \auSent{} frames they sent and the \auHeld{} verified copies they
kept of each other's frames over links losing \auLossPct\%, \auFrames{} frames in all.
Keeping the frames costs \auKept\,B per record.
That is slightly less than the \bprLeanDesign\,B of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}: the
mission's sender identifiers and sequence numbers are smaller than the table's sizing convention
assumes and take fewer CBOR bytes, which outweighs the longer key records later in the hour.

\runin{Delayed Frames}\label{sec:delay} The timestamp acceptance window refuses a frame whose
newest record is more than $\Delta$ older than the receiver's clock. With $\Delta = 150$\,ms, in
\rpFrames{} honest frames per setting, no frame was refused at clock errors up to 10\,ms, and every
frame held back by 250\,ms or more was refused. In a GNSS outage, two clocks drift apart by
\rpDrift\,ms per hour. A fixed window of 250\,ms then holds for \rpHolds{} minutes, and a window
widened by that drift keeps working, its bound growing at the same rate.
The window is held against a frame's newest record, taken as the frame leaves, so an
honest frame arrives \rpAfterMs\,ms old (channel and one verification), and \rpBudgetMs\,ms of a
250\,ms window remain for clock drift and the errors of the two clocks (\rpApartMs\,ms apart, one
standard deviation). After \rpHolds\,min without GNSS the drift is \rpDriftHold\,ms and the
margin \rpMarginMs\,ms, \rpMarginSd{} standard deviations: \rpHoldRefused{} of \rpFrames{} honest
frames was refused. After \rpNextMin\,min (\rpNextDrift\,ms of drift), \rpNextPct\% were.

\runin{Flooding}\label{sec:flooding} A forged frame --- with a real sender, a newer sequence
number and a well-formed signature under another key --- costs the Python receiver \flForgedMs\,ms
on the Pi~4, against \flHonestMs\,ms for an honest one, which also pays for its storage.
The flood uses \flSenders{} senders, one core's share of the \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes
for which the Python receiver needs \flCores{} cores (Section~\ref{sec:cpu}). Unattacked, they take \flQuietSharePct\% of the core and every honest frame is accepted,
whereas all \nmaxLeanDesign{} senders on one core leave $V = \flAllV$.
With \flSenders{} senders on one receiver core, \flNoneRate{} such frames per second push honest
delivery below 0.95 (Fig.~\ref{fig:flood}). In the Python receiver, the 8\,B group MAC is checked
before the frame is decoded and refuses an outsider's frame in \flTagUs\us. At 3\,000 forged
frames per second, more than the \flChannel{} that one channel carries, honest delivery stays at
\flOutsiderV. A member holding the key is stopped neither by the group MAC nor by a per-sender
rate cap: honest delivery falls below 0.95 from \flInsiderRate{} forged frames per second behind
the MAC, and from \flSpreadRate{} behind the cap when the forgeries name every sender. Against one
forging sender, the cap keeps delivery at \flCapOneV{} or more, up to the rate one channel
carries. The group MAC costs \flTagCostPct\% of capacity.

LoRaWAN limits the payload of a frame by data rate, and its EU863-870 plan defines twelve data
rates~\cite{lora_rp002}: seven LoRa (DR0--DR6), one FSK (DR7) and four LR-FHSS (DR8--DR11). By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl}, a self-verifying frame needs a header, a chain link, a signature and one key
record. The smallest such frame that the frame-linked format can produce at all --- sender 0,
sequence 0, every field zero --- is $\exFloorHdr + \linkField + 64 + \exFloorRec = \exFloor$\,B;
this frame was produced, signed and verified. Header, link and signature alone occupy
\exFloorNoRec\,B.
The header of this floor frame is \exFloorHdr\,B, one byte less than in
Table~\ref{tab:fields}: its record stream is a single \exFloorRec\,B record, and CBOR writes the
length of a byte string shorter than \exStreamLongAt\,B in its type byte.

Fig.~\ref{fig:excl} compares that floor with the payload limits. Three things follow:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \runin{Signature-Bound Rates} Five data rates (DR0--DR2, DR8 and DR10) allow 50 or 51\,B;
the signature alone does not fit.
\item \runin{Format-Bound Rates} Three data rates (DR3, DR9 and DR11) allow 115\,B; header, link
and signature fill the payload, and no record fits.
\item \runin{Feasible Rates} Four data rates (DR4--DR7) allow 222 or 242\,B and can carry a frame.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{\exclCountCap{} of twelve data rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame}, in either
format and under either of the payload tables of the standard. This exclusion bound does not
depend on the telemetry, since each rate is excluded against the floor of the format;
\exclFmtCount{} of the \exclCount{} depend on the format (condition~v below).

\runin{Scope} The result holds under five conditions. (i)~The limits are the regional parameters
of LoRaWAN; the LoRa PHY itself carries 255\,B~\cite{semtech_sx1276} and is not excluded.
(ii)~Every frame carries a public-key signature. (iii)~There is no loss recovery.
(iv)~$\epsilon \leq p$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} forbids spreading a signature over several
frames; at $p=0.01$ and $\epsilon=0.05$ a signed unit may span five frames, and the exclusion no
longer holds as stated. (v)~The frame is the format of Section~\ref{sec:frame}. The
\exclSigCount{} rates of 50 and 51\,B are excluded by the signature alone, whatever the format.
At 115\,B a signature and a 32\,B hash leave \exRoomAfterCrypto\,B, which the header and framing
of this format fill exactly. A hand-packed layout would leave part of them for a record, but less
than the \keyLean\,B that our key record averages.

\runin{Effect of Each Relaxation} Table~\ref{tab:excl} quantifies what each relaxation buys.
Taking the chain link off the air frees the three 115\,B rates: they are excluded by the link, not
by the signature. The smallest standardized signature, 48\,B BLS12-381~\cite{cfrg_bls}, fits a
51\,B payload with 3\,B to spare.
All BLS timings in this paper are of the 96\,B variant of blspy; the 48\,B variant was
not timed. It verifies with the same two pairings, and to leave the channel as the binding limit
at \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes it would have to verify in \blsVerNeedMs\,ms, \blsSpeedup{} times
faster than the \tVerBls\,ms measured.
At 115\,B, a frame whose fields are all zero would fit (\exFloorBls\,B), but the smallest frame of
our telemetry is \exLoBls\,B, a three-byte shortfall that is not counted as an exclusion. A 13\,B
symmetric tag, as in MAVLink~2, fits the 115\,B rates. At 50 or 51\,B, however, header and link
already take \exHdrLinkLo--\exHdrLinkHi\,B, so a hash-chained frame does not fit there under any
authenticator.

\runin{What the Feasible Rates Carry} At DR5 under the 1\% duty cycle, a 242\,B frame holds
\loraRecsLean{} records and sustains \loraRateLean~records/s per node (\loraRecsFirst{} records and
\loraRateFirst{} in the record-linked format). The batch must be sized at the record spacing it implies. At this rate the records of a frame
are \loraSpacingLean\,s apart (\loraSimSpacing\,s in the simulated frame below); their delta
records were sized at the nearest spacing measured, \loraSpacing\,s, where one costs
\deltaLeanFar\,B, not the \deltaLean\,B it costs at 50\,ms.
The ns-3 LoRaWAN module carries at most 222\,B, where a frame holds \loraSimRecs{} records.
Simulated with a \loraSimBytes\,B payload every \loraSimPeriod\,s (\loraSimRate~records/s per
node), on one channel with one demodulator and no capture, the largest neighborhood with mean
$V \geq 0.95$ is \loraNmax{} nodes (\seeds{} seeds, 95\% interval \loraNmaxCi; \loraNmaxRun{} if
95\% of runs must each reach it). The closed-form model of Haxhibeqiri
\emph{et al.}~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora}, fitted to hardware interference measurements and
evaluated at the same operating point, gives \loraNmaxExt. Such a neighborhood carries about
\loraAgg~records/s in all, where AUTHBC on 802.11 carries some \wifiAgg. On these links the
ledger therefore records provenance every few seconds; it is not the 802.11 design at a smaller
scale.

\runin{Stream Signing} Table~\ref{tab:stream} places the schemes of Section~\ref{sec:related} at
our operating point. Each was implemented and run in ns-3 in the frame-linked one-record frame,
with its own authenticator in place of the signature. All of them send a frame per record ---
\strFpsSig~frames/s per node (\strFpsEmss{} for EMSS, with its signature packets), where AUTHBC
sends \strFpsDesign{} --- so the fixed channel time of a frame is paid for every record, whatever
the size of the authenticator. A 13\,B tag in place of a 64\,B signature raises $N_{\max}$ from
\crossSig{} to \crossMav{} nodes but gives up non-repudiation; four records in a frame raise it
to \crossDesign. When a record counts only if it verifies within the 100\,ms deadline, only
MAVLink~2, Wong--Lam and TESLA with a short disclosure delay reach 95\%. Gennaro--Rohatgi and
every variant of EMSS never do, because a record waits for packets sent after it. Tree chaining
is the one scheme that keeps what a ledger requires --- every packet verifies alone, and its
origin can be shown to a third party --- and it pays \strAuthWl\,B per packet for it. These schemes
amortize the cost of signing; on a contended channel, the cost that binds is the frame.
Both capacity columns of Table~\ref{tab:stream} are crossings of 0.95, interpolated
between the two simulated sizes around it. In-time delivery is counted on the 20 sender--receiver
pairs traced in each run and delivery on all pairs, so the in-time crossing of Wong--Lam
(\sdWl) lies above its delivery crossing (\crossWl) by sampling alone, inside the interval
\crossCiWl.

\runin{Aggregate Signatures} The nearest published comparators are certificateless aggregate
signature schemes for vehicular broadcast. Table~\ref{tab:clas} lists what one signer sends per
message; for $n$ messages it is $n$ times as much, since each carries its own identity, key and
signature components. Aggregation acts afterwards, at a roadside unit: it reduces the work of the
verifier and what the unit forwards --- in the one scheme read at source, 388\,B for a single
signature and 784\,B for the aggregate of a hundred~\cite{wang2022clas}. Batching acts before, on
the own records of a sender. The two act on different links, and the table should not be read as
a ranking: those schemes provide conditional privacy, which AUTHBC does not; their group elements
are 128\,B where our signature is 64\,B; and their values are for a different message. What the
comparison does show is that per-message authentication of this kind costs several hundred
bytes, and that aggregation alone does not change that.

\subsection{What generalizes}
One insight carries beyond this paper: on a contended broadcast channel the cost that binds is
the frame, not the authenticator. A frame costs about \fixedCost\us{} of channel time whatever its
length, so schemes that amortize signing but send a frame per record gain little, and a
post-quantum signature leaves the gain of batching unchanged.

The same accounting applies to other broadcast systems (Table~\ref{tab:links}). ADS-B 1090ES and
Bluetooth~4 Remote~ID cannot carry a signature at all.
An ADS-B message carries 7\,B of data~\cite{strohmeier2015adsb} and a Bluetooth~4
Remote~ID message 25\,B; a message pack over Bluetooth~5 or Wi-Fi holds up to nine such
messages, 225\,B~\cite{rfc9575}.
Bluetooth~5 and Wi-Fi Remote~ID fit up to \olRidRecords{} records in a frame, but at one message a
second no frame meets any deadline up to 1\,s. V2X CAM at 10\,Hz admits no frame at 100\,ms,
\olCamTwo{} record at 200\,ms and \olCamThree{} at 300\,ms. This is consistent with those standards
signing each message, although it is not offered as their reason.

\subsection{Deployment recommendations}
Our findings give the following configuration guidance; the capacities behind it are simulated.
(1)~\emph{High-rate 802.11 swarms} ($\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$): one Ed25519 signature and one chain
link per four records, with a key record in every frame, at \bprLeanDesign\,B per record
(per-record signing: \bprLeanBase\,B); with the margin of (5), about \nmaxTightD{} nodes share
one collision domain. (2)~\emph{Tight deadlines}: per-record signing when
$1 < \Lambda D_{\max} \leq 2$, since no batch meets the deadline. When $\Lambda D_{\max} \leq 1$ no
frame meets it under the peak-age convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fresh}; a deployment that tolerates
one sample of staleness can use the wait $(b-1)/\Lambda$ instead. (3)~\emph{LoRaWAN provenance
links}: the four feasible data rates only, with the batch sized at the record spacing it implies.
On the other \exclCount{} rates a signed, chained, self-verifying frame cannot be carried;
Table~\ref{tab:excl} lists what each relaxation buys. (4)~\emph{Constrained receivers}: provision
the processor before the channel, since the Python receiver serves \ncpuProto{} nodes per core, a
Rust one \rxRustNodes, and BLS \ncpuBls. (5)~\emph{Loss budget}: set $\epsilon$ at least 0.02 above
the expected frame loss, since at $\epsilon = p$ the target is met with no margin
(Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}). (6)~\emph{Outsiders}: an 8\,B group MAC, at \flTagCostPct\% of capacity.
Table~\ref{tab:decision} summarizes these recommendations.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\runin{Capacities Are Simulated} On hardware, contention was measured between two radios only
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The capacities in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} come from ns-3 with one
collision domain, equal received power and static nodes. Unequal power with capture was simulated
separately and raises them (Section~\ref{sec:robust}); a swarm spread over a wider area, with
hidden stations, and a moving swarm were simulated as well, with no Doppler and no
time-correlated fading. Our traffic source randomizes each node's send time within its period. A
real autopilot is more regular: PX4's own stream, in simulation, holds its period to about
$\pm\sitlJitter$\,ms. That is nearer to the strictly periodic source, which gives the same mean
capacity but a wider spread between runs (Section~\ref{sec:sim}).

\runin{Telemetry Is Synthetic} Record sizes come from a generator. The check against flight logs
covers twelve logs of one autopilot at 5\,Hz position resolution, and simulated flights of that
autopilot at 50\,Hz, whose navigation-quality fields do not vary.

\runin{Loss Models} The loss experiment uses independent, Gilbert and length-dependent loss
generated at the frame level, and collision loss traced in ns-3; no loss was measured on air.
Under length-dependent loss, AUTHBC is below its target where one-record frames lose 5\%
(Section~\ref{sec:loss}). Batches of two, three and eight were simulated for the capacity law
alone, and nothing else here is evaluated at them.

\runin{Energy and CPU} Only the sender is metered, with a sensor never checked against a
reference load; receiver and radio energy are modeled. The CPU share of the receiver counts
verification and hashing, not decoding. Counting both, the Python prototype needs three of the
four cores of the board for the neighborhood of AUTHBC. The Rust receiver was timed alone,
without a radio or a dispatcher in the path.

\runin{Security Scope} Confidentiality, availability and key management are outside the design
(Section~\ref{sec:model}). A swarm member holding the group key can still flood, and a delayed
frame is refused only while GNSS time is available. The proofs use three separate models that are
not composed, and assume canonical encoding to be injective. With one link per frame, continuity
inside a frame rests on the frame signature alone, and showing one record to a third party means
showing its frame. The receiver therefore keeps every accepted frame, at its on-air length for
every $b$ records held.

\runin{Post-Quantum Signatures} Falcon-512 fits a frame (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). An ML-DSA
signature is 2420\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v} and exceeds the 1500\,B MTU with any header, so it cannot
travel in one frame at any batch size; freshness caps the batch at four, so it could not be
amortized either. Such a signature would need a design built around
fragmentation~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v,sobati2025fragmentsec}.

\runin{Multi-Hop} Relayed records are not evaluated. A relay cannot re-sign: it carries the
signatures of the originators or one BLS aggregate~\cite{bgls2003}. Both are implemented; neither
is measured here.

\subsection{Conclusion}
What a frame must carry decides where it can be sent. On a contended broadcast channel, the cost
that binds is the frame, not the authenticator. This work costs authentication at the frame rather
than at the record, using measured frames, ns-3 simulation with \seeds{} seeds per point and
metered sender energy.

Two feasibility boundaries result. Where the frame fits, one signature and one chain link per
four records cut the bytes per record by \saveLean\% and let one 802.11a collision domain carry
\nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes where per-record signing carries \nmaxLeanBase; the batch is capped by
freshness, not by the MTU, and every frame starts with a key record. Where it does not fit,
\exclCount{} of the twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN data rates cannot carry one self-verifying frame at
any batch size. The capacities are simulated; in a spread or moving swarm fewer neighbors are in
reach (at 1\,km, \spNeighbours{} with AUTHBC and \spNeighboursBase{} with per-record signing),
but the gain stays \spRatioLo--\mvRatioHi-fold. On hardware, contention was measured between two
radios only. In Rust, the frame-linked sender spends \enRustMeas\,$\mu$J per record,
a factor of \enFirstOverRust{} lower than the record-linked format with batching.

This accounting should carry over to other broadcast systems whose records are small beside
their authenticator, although this paper evaluates it for UAV telemetry only. Future work should
address the 5G NR sidelink, the 3GPP link for direct broadcast between vehicles, and a variant
for post-quantum signatures that do not fit one frame.

\section{AUTHBC frame layout}\label{app:frame}
For reproducibility, the complete layout of the frame-linked format is given here. A frame is one
canonical CBOR map with the integer keys 0--7:
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
frame = {
  0: 2,           ; v: format version
  1: 0 / 1 / 2,   ; t: frame type
  2: uint,        ; src: sender id (16 bit)
  3: uint,        ; base_seq (32 bit)
  4: 1..255,      ; n: records in frame
  5: bytes(32),   ; link: prev_hash of rec 0
  6: bytes,       ; recs: record stream
  7: bytes(64)    ; auth: one signature, or
     / [n * bytes(64)]     ; one per record
}
rec = 9 varints: ts lat lon alt
      vel_x vel_y vel_z battery mode
\end{verbatim}
}
\runin{Key Implementation Notes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Frame Types:} Type 0 carries one signature per record; type 1 carries one
signature and decodes alone; type 2 carries one signature and codes its first record against
the last record of the previous frame.
\item \emph{Record Coding:} A key record carries absolute values, with \texttt{ts},
\texttt{battery} and \texttt{mode} unsigned and the other fields zig-zag signed; a delta record
carries zig-zag differences from the record before it. All values are LEB128 variable-length
integers.
\item \emph{Derived Fields:} The sender identifier and sequence number of record $i$ are
\texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq}$\,+\,i$. Every \texttt{prev\_hash} after the first is
recomputed by the receiver and never read from the frame.
\item \emph{Signed Bytes:} A signature covers the canonical CBOR encoding of the rebuilt
ledger records, not the record stream.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why capacity is where it is: the derivation}\label{app:mechanism}
A frame is lost in this scenario in two ways. In the first, two stations that found the medium
busy draw the same backoff slot. With occupancy $\rho \approx N f T$ and a contention window of
$W = 16$ slots, counting the stations that wait together gives a loss of about
$\rho^2/(W(1-\rho))$. Hence $a = \rho/(W(1-\rho))$, which is \modelSlope{} at $\rho = \modelRho$,
the mean occupancy at which loss reaches 5\%. In the second, a station decides to send within the
4\us{} in which it cannot yet sense another: that is $c$, the vulnerable period
of~\cite{kleinrock1975csma}. Without the first-order step, the count
is $\rho\,[1-(1-1/W)^{\rho/(1-\rho)}]$; with the detection term and nothing fitted, solving it for
5\% gives the \modelCells{} capacities within \closedWorst\% (mean \closedMean\%).

The contention model is an event simulation of the access procedure alone, with nothing fitted
and none of the code of the simulator. Doubling the contention window moves the capacity of
per-record signing only from \modelBaseC{} to \modelNsWindowC{} nodes (predicted
\modelPredWindowC): stations that count down for longer wait together more often.
