\begin{abstract}
A UAV that broadcasts signed, hash-chained telemetry spends more bytes on authentication than on
data: a 64\,B signature and a 32\,B chain link accompany a record of about \keyLean\,B. AUTHBC
makes the frame the unit of design: a format, a sender and a receiver in which one signature and
one chain link cover $b$ records and every frame decodes and verifies alone. Batch size, encoding
and signature scheme are chosen together under freshness, verifiability, channel capacity and
receiver CPU. Two boundaries follow. Where the frame does not fit: when every frame must verify
alone, \exclCount{} of the twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN data rates cannot carry one. Where it does: at
50~records/s and a 100\,ms deadline the design sends \bprLeanDesign\,B per record against
\bprLeanBase\,B for per-record signing, and in ns-3 one 802.11a collision domain delivers 95\% of
frames from \nmaxLeanDesign{} neighbours instead of \nmaxLeanBase, found by direct search with a
bootstrap interval. Batching and header design decide what can run; among 64\,B elliptic-curve
signatures the scheme does not. Sender energy is measured on a Raspberry~Pi~4; contention is
simulated, and record sizes come from a synthetic generator checked against twelve public flight
logs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Unmanned aircraft in a swarm tell each other where they are many times a second. If those
reports are to be evidence afterwards --- of who was where, and of what each vehicle claimed ---
each one must be attributable to its sender and the sequence must be tamper-evident. The textbook
construction signs every record and links it to its predecessor by a hash. The textbook cost is
that the protection is larger than the thing protected: an Ed25519 or ECDSA signature is 64\,B and
a SHA-256 link is 32\,B, while a compactly encoded position, velocity and status record is about
\keyLean\,B. On a shared radio channel those bytes are airtime, and airtime decides how many
neighbours can be heard.

Practice today signs per message. MAVLink~2 appends a 13\,B symmetric tag to each
message~\cite{mavlink2signing}; vehicular standards attach an ECDSA signature, and periodically a
certificate, to each one~\cite{etsi_ts103097}. The obvious alternative is to let several records
share one frame, one signature and one chain link. Batching is not new, and its price is known in
kind: records wait for the frame to fill, and a lost frame loses all of them. What a designer
lacks is the accounting. What must a frame carry so that a receiver can verify it without any
other frame? On which links does such a frame not fit, whatever the batch? Where it fits, which
constraint caps the batch, and what do the saved bytes buy on a contended channel, in neighbours
and not in percent?

We answer these for one concrete system. AUTHBC --- authenticated telemetry for a
blockchain-style, hash-chained ledger --- is a frame format, a sender and a receiver. We built it,
measured it, and searched its parameters. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A frame as one object.} Every size we report is the length of a frame that was
emitted, decoded and verified (Sec.~\ref{sec:frame}). We give the field-level byte budget of two
formats: a first, text-keyed one and a lean one that is \leanVsFirstPct\% smaller.
\item \textbf{An exclusion with its scope.} On LoRaWAN, \exclCount{} of twelve EU863-870 data
rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame; we state the four conditions under which that
holds and what each relaxation buys (Sec.~\ref{sec:lowrate}).
\item \textbf{A ladder that separates causes.} Sharing a frame, sharing a signature and delta
coding are separate steps, and we report bytes, capacity, receiver CPU and energy for each
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). Capacity is found by simulating each configuration at its own frame size
and rate, with \seeds{} seeds and a bootstrap interval, not by applying one load ceiling to all.
\item \textbf{Constraints that bind in an unexpected order.} Freshness, not the MTU, caps the
batch at four records. A delta-coded frame that depends on its predecessor misses the
verifiability target at its own loss budget, so every frame carries one self-contained record.
One CPU core verifies \ncpuBatch{} neighbours' frames with Ed25519 and \ncpuBls{} with BLS.
\end{itemize}

The message is short: batching and header design decide what can run, and among standard 64\,B
elliptic-curve signatures the scheme does not change it. AUTHBC introduces no new cryptographic
primitive. What is measured, what is simulated and what is only modelled is stated with each
result and collected in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Per-message authentication.} MAVLink~2 signing appends a link identifier, a 48-bit
timestamp and a 48-bit truncated SHA-256 tag computed with a shared secret~\cite{mavlink2signing}.
It is cheap and it authenticates a \emph{link}: any holder of the key can produce any message, so
a record cannot be attributed to one vehicle afterwards. Rotta and Mykytyn likewise attach an
HMAC and a timestamp to each broadcast telemetry message of a swarm, noting how small such
messages are~\cite{rotta2024telemetrybroadcast}. Vehicular broadcast uses public-key
signatures: ECDSA over each message, with the signer's certificate in some messages and an
eight-octet digest of it in the others~\cite{etsi_ts103097,ndss2024pqv2v}. That is the trust model
we adopt, and the per-message cost we start from.

\textbf{Signing streams.} Amortising one signature over many packets is a classical problem.
Gennaro and Rohatgi sign the first packet and chain the rest by hashes~\cite{gennaro1997streams};
one lost packet breaks the chain. Wong and Lam sign a hash tree over a block and send in every
packet the hashes needed to reach the root~\cite{wong1999flows}, so each packet verifies alone;
the signing cost is amortised, the bytes are not. EMSS tolerates loss by carrying several hashes
per packet and a periodic signature packet, and TESLA replaces the signature by a MAC whose key
is disclosed later~\cite{perrig2000emss,perrig2002tesla}; TBRD applies TESLA to UAS Remote ID and
reports about half the overhead of signatures~\cite{veara2025tbrd}. These schemes spread
\emph{one authenticator across packets} and so make packets depend on each other or on a later
one. We do the opposite: we put \emph{several records in one packet}, which is possible only
because a telemetry record is a small fraction of a frame. Each frame then stays self-verifying
and the cost moves to latency, at the sender. TESLA delays verification at the receiver instead,
and a disclosed MAC key gives no non-repudiation, which a ledger needs.
Section~\ref{sec:clas} places these schemes at our operating point.

\textbf{Aggregate and batch verification.} BLS signatures aggregate~\cite{bls2001,bgls2003}, and
certificateless aggregate schemes (CLAS) for vehicular networks build on such
constructions~\cite{li2025clas}. Zhang \emph{et al.} verify many signatures at once at a
roadside unit~\cite{zhang2008batch}. Both reduce the verifier's \emph{work}; each message still
carries its own signature material, so neither reduces what is sent. Section~\ref{sec:clas}
compares bytes.

\textbf{Hash-linked records.} Linking each record to the hash of its predecessor to make a
sequence tamper-evident is due to Haber and Stornetta~\cite{haber1991timestamp} and is the chain
in every blockchain~\cite{nakamoto2008}; UAV uses are surveyed in~\cite{mehta2020blockchainuav}.
We take the chain as given and ask what it costs on air.

\textbf{Small payloads.} That a signature leaves little room in a small frame is known:
G{\"u}ndo{\u{g}}an \emph{et al.} find 9\,B of application data left beside an Ed25519 signature in
an 802.15.4 frame~\cite{gundogan2021firmware}, and post-quantum signatures do not fit one 5G
broadcast block~\cite{arxiv2510pqsib1}. Section~\ref{sec:lowrate} applies the same arithmetic to
LoRaWAN with the chain link included. Compact encodings~\cite{rfc8949} and delta coding of time
series~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} are established; a lossy link limits how far a delta chain may
run, which Sec.~\ref{sec:loss} quantifies.

\textbf{Channel models.} Bianchi's model gives the saturation throughput of the 802.11
DCF~\cite{bianchi2000}. Broadcast frames are not acknowledged and the contention window never
doubles, which changes the fixed point; Ma and Chen give the broadcast
model~\cite{machen2007,machen2008}, which we use and validate against ns-3~\cite{nsnam}. For
LoRa, Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.} model single-gateway scalability~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora} and
Zirak \emph{et al.} report air-to-air delivery against range on hardware~\cite{zirak2021swarm}.

\section{System and Threat Model}\label{sec:model}
\input{fig_system}

\textbf{Network and traffic.} $N$ UAVs share one channel and hear each other: one collision
domain. Each produces $\Lambda$ telemetry records per second and broadcasts them; there are no
acknowledgements and no retransmissions. A record holds a millisecond timestamp, latitude,
longitude and altitude, three velocity components, battery level and flight mode, all integers.
Our primary operating point is $\Lambda = 50$~records/s with a freshness bound
$D_{\max} = 100$\,ms: the rate is PX4's companion-computer stream rate~\cite{px4_mavlink_streams}
and the pair meets the direct UAV-to-UAV broadcast requirements of
3GPP~\cite{3gpp_ts22125}. A relaxed point, 20~records/s and 250\,ms, is reported for comparison.

\textbf{Ledger.} Each UAV keeps an append-only chain of its own records,
$\mathit{prev\_hash}_{i} = \mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{rec}_{i-1})$, and stores the verified
records it hears from others. There is no consensus layer; the subject of this paper is
authenticated dissemination.

\textbf{Adversary.} The adversary controls the radio: it reads, injects, replays, modifies,
delays and drops frames, and may operate many radios. It holds no honest UAV's private key.

\textbf{What is protected.} (i)~\emph{Origin and integrity:} a stored record was produced by the
UAV it names and was not altered, by a public-key signature checked before anything is stored.
(ii)~\emph{Replay:} sequence numbers are strictly increasing per sender; a repeated frame is
refused. (iii)~\emph{Equivocation:} two validly signed records for one sequence number are kept
as a pair, which is transferable evidence because both carry the sender's signature.
(iv)~\emph{Continuity:} omission or reordering within what a receiver holds is detected through
the chain.

\textbf{What is not.} Telemetry is sent in clear. Jamming is not resisted: loss appears as the
robustness measure $V$ below, not as a security property. Replay is refused by sequence number,
not by time, so a frame that an attacker withholds and delivers later is accepted, authentic and
in order, however stale; refusing it needs a clock shared with the sender, which we do not assume.
A UAV with a valid key can sign false data; only inconsistent falsehood is detectable. Key
distribution, revocation and admission are assumed: receivers hold senders' public keys, and we
charge the bytes of delivering certificates separately (Sec.~\ref{sec:certs}). Senders are not
anonymous.

\textbf{Verifiability.} $V$ is the probability that a record a UAV generates is received,
decoded and verified by a given neighbour. The requirement is $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon = 0.05$.

A frame is one canonical CBOR map~\cite{rfc8949} with integer keys (Table~\ref{tab:fields}).
\texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq} name the sender and the sequence number of the first record;
\texttt{link} is the hash of the record before it; \texttt{recs} is the record stream and
\texttt{auth} one signature. A record in the stream is nine variable-length integers. The first
record of a frame is a \emph{keyframe}, carrying absolute values (\keyLean\,B on average); each
later one is a \emph{delta} from the record before it (\deltaLean\,B when records are 50\,ms
apart). Sender and sequence number are not repeated in the records.

\textbf{What is signed} is not the stream but the canonical ledger records, each with its own
\texttt{prev\_hash}. The receiver rebuilds them --- the first \texttt{prev\_hash} is
\texttt{link}, each later one is the hash of the record just rebuilt --- and only then checks the
signature. A frame that cannot be decoded therefore cannot be verified, and nothing unverified is
stored. Each verified record is offered to the ledger store, which refuses a sequence number that
is not newer, keeps an equivocating pair as evidence, and flags a link that contradicts a record
it holds. The state against which a later frame would be decoded advances only after a frame has
been verified and stored, so a replayed frame cannot desynchronise a stream. Flipping any single
bit of a frame makes verification fail (all \bitflips{} positions of a four-record frame were
tried).

\textbf{When a frame is lost,} the next one still decodes and verifies, because it starts with a
keyframe. Its \texttt{link} then names a record this receiver does not hold: the gap shows in the
sequence numbers, and the link is stored unchecked, to be checked by any party that holds the
missing record. A late copy of the lost frame is refused, since sequence numbers must increase;
the gap is not repaired.

\textbf{One link per frame.} Inside a frame the order and content of records rest on the frame
signature alone; in the first format below each record also carries an independent hash. A frame
is signed and accepted as a unit, so no strength is lost, but one mechanism remains where there
were two.

\textbf{The first format.} For comparison we keep the format we built first: text keys, and in
every record its own chain link, sender identifier and sequence number. Its header is
\hdrFirst\,B, of which \keyNamesFirst\,B are key names. That format is implemented and frozen;
its delta-coded frames, however, are a sum of separately measured parts and were never emitted,
and we mark them as a byte model wherever they appear.

\textbf{Design parameters.} A configuration is the batch size $b$; whether a signature covers
each record (\emph{inline}; \texttt{auth} is then an array of $b$ signatures) or the frame
(\emph{batch}); the signature scheme (Ed25519~\cite{ed25519} and ECDSA P-256~\cite{fips186} at
64\,B, BLS12-381~\cite{bls2001} at 96\,B in the library used); and the reference interval $r$:
one frame in $r$ starts with a keyframe, the others with a delta against the previous frame.

\section{Constraints}\label{sec:constraints}

\textbf{Bytes.} With header $H_f$, link field $L$, signature \ba, keyframe $s_k$ and delta
$s_\delta$, a self-contained batch frame costs, per record,
\begin{equation}
B(b) = \frac{H_f + L + \ba + s_k + (b-1)\,s_\delta}{b}.
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
$B(1)$ is our baseline: one record, one signature, one frame. Signing each record of a shared
frame replaces \ba{} by $b\,\ba$. Equation~\eqref{eq:bytes} is exact to within a byte of CBOR length
prefix; the sizes we report are those of emitted frames.

\textbf{Freshness.} The oldest record in a frame waits for the frame to fill. Adding the
channel-access and airtime delay $T_{ch}$ and the verification time $t_v$,
\begin{equation}
D(b) = \frac{b}{\Lambda} + T_{ch} + t_v \;\leq\; D_{\max}.
\label{eq:fresh}
\end{equation}
Fill time dominates. At both operating points $\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$: five records fill in exactly
$D_{\max}$ and leave nothing for the channel, so $b \leq 4$ whatever the encoding or the scheme.
The 1500\,B MTU would admit \mtuRecsFirst{} records in the first format and \mtuRecsLean{} in
the lean one.

\textbf{Verifiability.} If one frame in $r$ is self-contained, frame $j$ of a group verifies only
if it and the $j-1$ frames before it arrive. With independent frame loss $p$,
\begin{equation}
V(r,p) = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-p)^{j},
\label{eq:v}
\end{equation}
which equals $1-p$ at $r=1$ and is smaller for every $r>1$. A target $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon \leq p$ therefore admits $r=1$ only. The same argument forbids splitting a signed unit
over $n$ frames, which verifies with probability $(1-p)^n$.

\textbf{Channel.} For a configuration --- a frame size and a frame rate per node --- $N_{\max}$
is the largest neighbourhood in which the mean delivered fraction of frames is at least
$1-\epsilon$. We find it by simulation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). As a conservative companion we
give $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$, the largest $N$ whose offered frame rate does not exceed the saturation
throughput of the broadcast DCF model~\cite{machen2008}.

\textbf{Receiver CPU.} A receiver verifies every neighbour's frames. With $k$ signatures per frame,
verification time $t_v$ and hash time $t_h$, one neighbour costs
$(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h$ seconds of CPU per second, and one core serves
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{cpu}} = 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:cpu}
\end{equation}
nodes.

\textbf{Payload.} A frame that verifies alone must fit the link's maximum payload $M$:
\begin{equation}
M \;\geq\; H_f + L + \ba + s_k .
\label{eq:excl}
\end{equation}
The residual room beside a signature has been computed before for
802.15.4~\cite{gundogan2021firmware}; here the chain link and a record that decodes alone are
charged as well.

\textbf{Relayed records.} A node that forwards records of $b$ different originators cannot
re-sign them. It carries their $b$ signatures, $64\,b$\,B, or one BLS aggregate of
96\,B~\cite{bgls2003}, saving $64 - 96/b$\,B per record at the price of a pairing-based
verification. Both placements are implemented. We evaluate only a sender's own records;
multi-hop forwarding is outside this paper.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\textbf{Implementation.} Formats, sender, receiver and store are one Python code base with four
record encodings (JSON, CBOR, MessagePack, delta) and three signature schemes behind common
interfaces, each scheme checked against published test vectors (for Ed25519,
those of~\cite{rfc8032}). Every table in this
paper is generated from result files that the code writes; a gate re-derives the deterministic ones
on each commit.

\textbf{Telemetry.} Records come from a seeded random-walk generator: \seeds{} seeds of 1000
records at 50\,ms spacing. Header fields are sized for a five-digit sender identifier one hour into
a 50\,Hz flight. Section~\ref{sec:px4} checks the generator against flight logs.

\textbf{Timing and energy.} Signing, verification, hashing and encoding are timed on a
Raspberry~Pi~4 (medians; Ed25519 sign/verify \tSignEd/\tVerEd\us, ECDSA \tSignEc/\tVerEc\us, BLS
\tSignBls/\tVerBls\,ms, SHA-256 \tHash\us). Sender energy is metered on the same board with an
INA219 on the supply rail, five repetitions per configuration, idle power subtracted.

\textbf{Channel simulation.} ns-3.48~\cite{nsnam}: IEEE~802.11a at 6\,Mb/s, ad hoc mode,
broadcast frames, all nodes in range at equal received power, so there is no capture and no
hidden terminal. Each node sends one frame every $b/\Lambda$ seconds. Its send instant is drawn uniformly within each period, so the relative phases of the nodes change from frame to frame. A strictly periodic source freezes each node's phase for a whole run. A run then samples a
single phase configuration, in which a few nodes may collide in every period while the others
lose nothing; over \srcPoints{} points run under both sources the mean delivered fraction
differs by \srcMeanDiff, and the capacities read from the strictly periodic source are
\srcShiftLo--\srcShiftHi\% lower and far less precise. A run
lasts 20\,s; each point is \seeds{} runs. $N_{\max}$ is the largest simulated $N$ whose mean
delivered fraction is at least 0.95 with every smaller simulated $N$ also passing; its interval
is the 2.5--97.5 percentile of that quantity over 10\,000 bootstrap resamples of the seeds. The
saturation models were validated separately against saturated ns-3 runs
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

LoRaWAN limits the payload of a frame by data rate, and its EU863-870 plan defines twelve data
rates~\cite{lora_rp002}. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl} a frame that verifies alone needs a header, a
chain link, a signature and one record that decodes alone. The smallest such frame the lean
format can emit at all --- sender 0, sequence 0, every field zero --- is
$\exFloorHdr + \linkField + 64 + \exFloorRec = \exFloor$\,B, and we emitted, signed and verified
it. Header, link and signature alone are \exFloorNoRec\,B.

Figure~\ref{fig:excl} sets that against the payload limits. Five data rates (DR0--DR2, DR8, DR10)
allow 50 or 51\,B: the signature alone does not fit. Three (DR3, DR9, DR11) allow 115\,B: header,
link and signature fill the payload and no record fits. Four (DR4--DR7) allow 222 or 242\,B and
are feasible. \textbf{\exclCountCap{} of twelve data rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame}, in
either format and under either of the standard's payload tables. None of the eight depends on the
telemetry, since each is excluded against the format's floor.

\textbf{Scope.} The result holds under four conditions. (i)~The limits are LoRaWAN's regional
parameters; the LoRa PHY itself carries 255\,B~\cite{semtech_sx1276} and is not excluded.
(ii)~Every frame carries a public-key signature. (iii)~There is no loss recovery. (iv)~$\epsilon
\leq p$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} forbids spreading a signature over several frames; at
$p=0.01$ and $\epsilon=0.05$ a signed unit may span five frames and the exclusion no longer holds
as stated.

\textbf{What each relaxation buys} (Table~\ref{tab:excl}). Taking the chain link off the air
frees the three 115\,B rates: they are excluded by the link, not by the signature. The smallest
standardised signature, 48\,B BLS12-381~\cite{cfrg_bls}, fits a 51\,B payload and leaves 3\,B; at
115\,B a frame of zeros would fit (\exFloorBls\,B) but the smallest frame of our telemetry is
\exLoBls\,B, a three-byte miss that we do not count as an exclusion. A 13\,B symmetric tag, as in
MAVLink~2, fits the 115\,B rates, but at 50 or 51\,B header and link already take
\exHdrLinkLo--\exHdrLinkHi\,B: a hash-chained frame does not fit there under any authenticator.

\textbf{What the feasible rates carry.} At DR5 under the 1\% duty cycle a 242\,B frame holds
\loraRecsLean{} records and sustains \loraRateLean~records/s per node (\loraRecsFirst{} records
and \loraRateFirst{} in the first format). The batch must be sized at the record spacing it
implies: a delta that spans \loraSpacing\,s costs \deltaLeanFar\,B, not the \deltaLean\,B it
costs at 50\,ms. Simulated with the ns-3 LoRaWAN module on one channel with one demodulator and
no capture, the largest neighbourhood with mean $V \geq 0.95$ is \loraNmax{} nodes (95\%
interval \loraNmaxCi; \loraNmaxRun{} if 95\% of runs must each reach it); the closed-form
model of Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.}~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora}, fitted to hardware interference
measurements and evaluated at the same operating point, gives \loraNmaxExt. Such a neighbourhood
carries about \loraAgg~records/s in all, where the 802.11 design of
Section~\ref{sec:results} carries some \wifiAgg. On these
links a ledger is a slow record of provenance, not a smaller version of the same design.

\section{Results on 802.11}\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} takes the design apart. A baseline that signs every record and sends it
alone confounds two things, so rows 2 and 6 separate them: four records share a frame but each
keeps its signature. In the lean format that step takes \bprLeanBase\,B per record to
\bprLeanInline: the header and the chain link are paid once per four records, and the three
records that follow the first can be coded as deltas. Sharing the signature as well gives
\textbf{\bprLeanDesign\,B, \saveLean\% below the baseline}; row~7 is the same frame without
delta coding, \bprLeanKeys\,B. In the first format the steps are sharing the frame
(\bprFirstBase{} to \bprFirstInline\,B), sharing the signature (\bprFirstCbor) and delta coding
(\bprFirstDesign, \saveFirst\% below its baseline). It ends higher because each of its records
carries a 32\,B link of its own, which no batch size amortises.

An exhaustive search over placement, batch size 1--12 and reference interval returns row~8 as the
byte-minimal configuration that meets the freshness, verifiability and MTU constraints, and
row~4 for the first format. The batch is set by freshness (Eq.~\ref{eq:fresh}), not by the MTU.

The choices are made together because they are not independent. One signature per frame saves
$\ba(1-1/b)$ per record, and delta coding saves $(1-1/b)$ of the difference between a
self-contained record and a delta, since each frame must start with a self-contained one. Both
vanish at $b=1$, where rows 1 and 5 already are the design. Batching is the choice the other two
gains depend on, and the deadline sets it.

Signature scheme does not move these rows among the 64\,B schemes: Ed25519 and ECDSA produce
identical frames. BLS adds 32\,B per frame and, as shown below, fails on CPU.

Delta coding is usually run with a keyframe every $K$ records. With $K=16$ and $b=4$ only one
frame in four decodes without its predecessor, and a lost frame takes the following ones with it.
Table~\ref{tab:loss} measures this with the codec in the loop: at the loss rate the system is
specified for, $r=4$ verifies \lossVFour{} of records, not 0.95. Equation~\eqref{eq:v} predicts
the measurement at every $r$. Bursty loss is kinder to a dependent design --- a Gilbert process
with the same mean and bursts of four frames gives \lossVFourBurst{} at $r=4$ --- but never
reaches $1-p$. A cleaner link would admit a longer interval ($r=4$ at $p=0.02$, $r=8$ at
$p=0.01$), and the whole prize is \lossPrize\,B per record. The design therefore uses $r=1$:
each frame starts with a keyframe, costs \bprLeanDesign\,B per record, and loses only its own
records when it is lost.

These figures give every frame the same loss probability. At a fixed bit error rate a longer
frame is lost more often, and a batch is a longer frame: on a channel that loses 5\% of the
baseline's one-record frames, the design's frame is lost with probability \lossBerP, so
\lossBerTh{} of its records are expected to verify (measured: \lossBerV~\lossBerCi) --- below
the target it meets at equal frame loss. It reaches 0.95 only while one-record frames are lost
at most \lossBerPmax{} of the time. The comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} are at equal frame loss
and carry that qualification.

Bytes matter because they are airtime. Figure~\ref{fig:nmax} shows delivery falling as

The lean baseline holds \nmaxLeanBase{} neighbours at 95\% delivery and the design

moves with the operating point --- \ratioLean$\times$ and \ratioRelLean$\times$ in the lean
format, \ratioFirst$\times$ and \ratioRelFirst$\times$ in the first --- and it depends on the
format, so we quote it with the format and not as one number.

\textbf{A rule for the crossing.} At the 5\% level, loss follows a line in airtime. With
$f = \Lambda/b$ the frames per second a node sends and $T$ the time one frame holds the medium
(its airtime plus DIFS),
\begin{equation}
N_{\max} \approx \frac{0.05}{f\,(a\,T + c)}, \qquad a = \lineA,\quad c = 8\,\mu\mathrm{s},
\label{eq:line}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is twice the simulator's preamble-detection time and $a$ was fitted to six rows of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8), which it reproduces within \lineFitWorst\%. We
wrote down what the rule gives for seven other configurations --- rows 3 and 7, the design with
one keyframe in four frames, and both formats at the relaxed point --- before simulating them.
All seven fell within \lineWorst\% of it. The shortcut we had used before, one utilisation
ceiling measured at a single point, misses the thirteen by up to \lineCeilWorst\%, in both
directions. Equation~\eqref{eq:line} is a fit at one loss level and one PHY rate, for frames of
146--565\,B, 5--50~frames/s and \lineNlo--\lineNhi{} nodes; the tables quote simulated values,
not the rule.


\textbf{Receiver CPU.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpu} one core of the Raspberry~Pi~4 verifies the frames
of \ncpuBatch{} neighbours when each frame carries one Ed25519 signature and of \ncpuInline{} when
every record is signed. At its $N_{\max}$ the design uses \cpuLeanDesign\% of a core, so the
channel binds first. Row~6 shows the constraint is not idle: with a signature on every record it
reaches the channel's limit at \cpuLeanInline\% of a core. With BLS a verification takes \tVerBls\,ms and one core serves
\ncpuBls{} neighbours: on this hardware BLS is excluded by CPU long before the channel is
reached, in addition to its larger signature. These figures verify one signature at a time.
Ed25519 was designed for batch verification --- its authors report under 134\,000 cycles per
signature in a batch of 64, against 273\,364 for one~\cite{ed25519} --- which would roughly
halve the receiver's cost; we did not use it, and ECDSA does not admit it.

\textbf{Other PHY rates.} We simulated one rate. Evaluating the saturation bound with the same
model at 12 and 24\,Mb/s, and on a 10\,MHz channel at 3--12\,Mb/s, gives design-to-baseline
ratios of $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$ from \phyRatioLo$\times$ to \phyRatioHi$\times$, lowest at the
slowest rate, because the fixed cost per frame does not shrink with the payload.

\subsection{Certificates}\label{sec:certs}
The rows so far assume that receivers already hold the senders' public keys. Vehicular practice
sends a full certificate in every fifth message and an eight-octet digest in the
others~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}. Charging that to every frame --- a \certBytes\,B certificate, so
\certPerFrame\,B per frame on average --- gives the ``with cert.'' column of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder}: \certLeanDesign\,B per record for the design against \certLeanBase{} for
the baseline. Certificates are a per-frame cost, so batching amortises them too.

\subsection{Is the synthetic telemetry representative?}\label{sec:px4}
Every size above comes from one generator, whose delta record is exactly \deltaLean\,B in every
sample --- the floor of the format. We ran twelve public PX4 flight logs (four quadrotors, two
hexa/octorotors, three fixed wings, three VTOLs; \pxRecords{} in-flight records), chosen by a rule
fixed before any was opened, through the same encoder. The logs carry position at 5\,Hz, so the
comparison is at 0.2\,s spacing and above, not at the 50\,Hz of the operating point. At 0.2\,s a
real delta record averages \pxDelta\,B against the generator's \pxDeltaGen\,B, and a real
keyframe \pxKey\,B against \pxKeyGen. The spread is by speed: the six multicopters, mostly
hovering, average \pxDeltaSlow\,B and the three fixed wings up to \pxDeltaFast\,B. With real
sizes at 0.2\,s the design saves \pxSave\% (\pxSaveWorst\% in the least favourable log) where
the generator gives \pxSaveGen\%. The generator is representative to within about one
percentage point, and optimistic for fast vehicles.

\subsection{Validation of the models}\label{sec:validation}
The unicast DCF model~\cite{bianchi2000} matches saturated ns-3 runs within
$+\valUniHi$/$-\valUniLo$\% and the broadcast model~\cite{machen2008} within
$\pm\valBcast$\% over $N=5$--50 (\seeds{} seeds). On two Raspberry~Pi~4 radios with a single
transmitter, the airtime of a 1400\,B broadcast frame measures \hwAirtime\,ms against a predicted
\hwAirtimeModel\,ms (\hwAirtimeGap\%) and \hwLossFrames{} frames in \hwSentFrames{} are lost
($p = \hwLoss$). That experiment has no contention. The capacities in this paper rest on
simulation.

\textbf{Stream signing.} Table~\ref{tab:stream} places the schemes of Section~\ref{sec:related}

that scheme's authenticator, sized from its definition, in place of the signature. All of them

the authenticator weighs. A 13\,B tag in place of a 64\,B signature raises the saturation bound
from \strNsatSig{} to \strNsatMav{} neighbours and gives up non-repudiation; four records in a frame
raise it to \strNsatDesign. Tree chaining is the one scheme that keeps what a ledger needs
--- every packet verifies alone and its origin can be shown to a third party --- and it pays \strAuthWl\,B per packet for
it. These schemes amortise the cost of signing; on a contended channel the cost that binds is
the frame.

\textbf{Aggregate signatures.} The nearest published comparators are certificateless aggregate signature schemes for vehicular
broadcast. Table~\ref{tab:clas} lists the per-message size each reports. In every one the cost
for $n$ messages is $n$ times the cost of one: aggregation reduces the verifier's work, while
each message still carries its own identity, key and signature components. Batching reduces what
is sent. The two act on different quantities, and the table should not be read as a ranking:
those schemes provide conditional privacy, which AUTHBC does not; their group elements are
128\,B where our signature is 64\,B; and their figures are for a different message. What the
comparison does show is that per-message authentication of this kind costs several hundred
bytes, against which a telemetry record is small, and that aggregation alone does not change
that.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Contention is simulated.} The hardware experiment has one transmitter. The capacities in
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} come from ns-3 with one collision domain, equal received power and static
nodes; capture, hidden terminals, spatial reuse and mobility are absent. Send instants are
redrawn within each period; how regular a real autopilot's stream is was not measured, and a
strictly periodic source gives capacities up to \srcShiftHi\% lower with a much wider spread
between runs. A contention
experiment on three or four radios is the next measurement.

\textbf{Telemetry is synthetic.} Record sizes come from a generator. The check against flight
logs covers twelve logs of one autopilot at 5\,Hz position resolution; nothing was measured at
50\,Hz.

\textbf{One PHY, generated loss.} Capacity was simulated at 6\,Mb/s only. The loss experiment
uses independent, Gilbert and length-dependent loss, all generated at the frame level, not a
channel trace; under the last, the design is below its target where the baseline's frame loses
5\% (Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}). The stream-signing schemes of Table~\ref{tab:stream} are modelled,
not implemented.

\textbf{Energy.} Only the sender is metered, and only the first format; receiver and radio energy
are modelled.

\textbf{Security scope.} Confidentiality, availability, key management and protection against a
delaying attacker are outside the design (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}). With one link per frame,
continuity inside a frame rests on the frame signature alone.

\textbf{Post-quantum signatures.} An ML-DSA signature is 2420\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v} and exceeds
the 1500\,B MTU with any header, so it cannot ride in one frame at any batch size; freshness caps
the batch at four, so it could not be amortised either. A post-quantum variant would be a
different design, built around fragmentation~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v,sobati2025fragmentsec}.

\textbf{Multi-hop.} Relayed records and more than one collision domain are not evaluated.

\section{Conclusion}
For small telemetry records the frame, not the record, is the right unit of authentication, and
what a frame must carry decides where it can be sent. A frame that verifies alone needs a header,
a chain link, a signature and one self-contained record: \exclCount{} of twelve LoRaWAN data
rates cannot carry that, at any batch size. Where the frame fits, one signature and one chain
link per four records cut the bytes per record by \saveLean\% and let one 802.11a collision
domain carry \nmaxLeanDesign{} neighbours where per-record signing carries \nmaxLeanBase. Two
constraints shape the design and are easy to get wrong: the batch is capped by freshness, not by
the MTU, and a frame that depends on its predecessor does not meet a verifiability target equal
to the loss rate. Batching and header design decide what can run; among 64\,B signatures the
scheme does not. The capacity figures are simulated and await a contention experiment on
hardware.
