\begin{abstract}
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) that broadcast signed, hash-chained telemetry spend more bytes
on authentication than on data: a 64\,B signature and a 32\,B chain link accompany a
\keyLean\,B record. This paper presents AUTHBC, a frame format, sender and receiver in which one
signature and one chain link cover $b$ records while every frame decodes and verifies alone.
Batch size, encoding and signature scheme are selected jointly under four constraints:
freshness, verifiability, channel capacity and receiver CPU. The evaluation combines emitted
frames, ns-3 simulation and metered sender energy. The results demonstrate two feasibility
boundaries. First, \exclCount{} of twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN data rates cannot carry one
self-verifying frame (\exclSigCount{} cannot hold the signature in any format, \exclFmtCount{}
our frame). Second, at 50~records/s and a 100\,ms deadline the design sends
\bprLeanDesign\,B per record against \bprLeanBase\,B for per-record signing, and one simulated
802.11a collision domain delivers 95\% of frames among \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes instead of
\nmaxLeanBase. Batching gains nothing unless record rate times deadline exceeds two. Capacities
are simulated, including a spread, moving swarm (gain \spRatioLo--\mvRatioHi-fold); contention
was measured on two radios.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Flying Ad-hoc Networks (FANETs) enable Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) swarms to coordinate
without centralized infrastructure, and each vehicle in a swarm reports its position, velocity
and status to its neighbors many times per second. When these reports must serve as evidence
afterwards --- of where each vehicle was, and of what each vehicle claimed --- every report must
be attributable to its sender and the sequence must be tamper-evident. The standard construction
signs every record and links it to its predecessor by a hash. However, this construction
introduces a critical challenge once the payload is compact: the protection becomes larger than
the data it protects. Our earlier comparison of encoding formats showed that canonical Concise
Binary Object Representation (CBOR) reduces an 18-field telemetry message from 358\,B in
JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) to 130\,B~\cite{farouk2026encoding}. The compactly encoded
position, velocity and status record of this work occupies approximately \keyLean\,B, whereas an
Ed25519 or Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) signature occupies 64\,B and a
SHA-256 chain link 32\,B. On a shared radio channel these bytes translate directly into
airtime, and airtime determines how many neighbors can be heard.

Current practice authenticates each message individually. MAVLink~2 appends a 13\,B symmetric
tag to each message~\cite{mavlink2signing}, and vehicular standards attach an ECDSA signature,
and periodically a certificate, to each one~\cite{etsi_ts103097}. Existing stream-signing
schemes~\cite{gennaro1997streams,wong1999flows,perrig2000emss} amortize the cost of signing
across packets without reducing the number of frames that a sender places on the channel.
Conversely, aggregate-signature schemes~\cite{li2025clas} reduce the work of the verifier
without reducing what each signer transmits. Letting several records share one frame, one
signature and one chain link is an established alternative, and its price is known in kind:
records wait for the frame to fill, and a lost frame loses all of them. What we did not find is
the accounting: what a frame must carry so that a receiver can verify it without any other
frame, on which links such a frame does not fit at any batch size, which constraint caps the
batch where it fits, and what the saved bytes buy on a contended channel in nodes and not in
percent. This gap leaves system designers without data-driven guidance for selecting batch
size, encoding and signature scheme jointly.

This paper presents AUTHBC --- authenticated telemetry for a blockchain-style,
hash-chained ledger --- as a frame format, a sender and a receiver. The system was implemented,
measured, and searched over its parameters. The main contributions of this work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A capacity rule with its mechanism. Capacity is obtained by simulating each
configuration at its own frame size and rate, with \seeds{} seeds and a bootstrap interval, and
not by applying one load ceiling to all. A rule with one fitted constant, the capacity envelope
of the title, reproduces the simulated capacities. The access rule accounts for the constant
and, with one loss mechanism that earlier analyses of broadcast do not contain, yields the
capacities with nothing fitted. Every prediction made from either was written down before its
runs, other batch sizes and unequal received power among them (Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}).
\item A ladder that separates causes. Sharing a frame, sharing a signature and delta coding
are separate steps; bytes, capacity and receiver CPU are reported for each
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), and sender energy with and without batching. Most of the capacity is
gained by sending fewer frames, the effect that frame aggregation exists for.
\item An exclusion bound with its scope. On LoRaWAN, \exclCount{} of twelve EU863-870 data
rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame: \exclSigCount{} because the signature alone does
not fit, and \exclFmtCount{} because of the header of this frame. The five conditions under
which the bound holds are stated, together with what each relaxation buys
(Sec.~\ref{sec:lowrate}).
\item A frame as one object, and the constraint that binds. The design and its baseline are
frames that were emitted, decoded and verified, and their sizes are measured lengths
(Sec.~\ref{sec:frame}); the lean format is \leanVsFirstPct\% smaller than the format we built
first. Freshness, not the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU), caps the batch at four records. A
delta-coded frame that depends on its predecessor misses the verifiability target at its own
loss budget, so every frame carries one self-contained record. Counting cryptography alone, one
CPU core serves a neighborhood of \ncpuBatch{} nodes with Ed25519 and of \ncpuBls{} with BLS;
the interpreted receiver we built serves \ncpuProto{} on one core, and its compiled version
\rxRustNodes, so that the channel, not the processor, binds.
\item A formal model and two defenses. Origin, integrity, replay protection, equivocation
evidence, gap detection and continuity are proven in Tamarin, and two gaps of the threat model
are closed: a GNSS-time window refuses delayed frames, and an 8\,B group tag refuses the frames
of outsiders before they cost a verification (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}).
\end{itemize}

In summary, batching and header design decide what can run, and among standard 64\,B
elliptic-curve signatures the choice of scheme does not. AUTHBC introduces no new cryptographic
primitive. Each result states whether it is measured, simulated or only modeled, and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} collects the limitations.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Our work intersects per-message authentication, stream signing, aggregate and batch
verification, hash-linked records, compact encoding for small payloads, and broadcast channel
models.

\runin{Per-Message Authentication} MAVLink~2 signing appends a link identifier, a 48-bit
timestamp and a 48-bit truncated SHA-256 tag computed with a shared
secret~\cite{mavlink2signing}. This mechanism authenticates a \emph{link}: any holder of the key
can produce any message, so a record cannot be attributed to one vehicle afterwards. Rotta and
Mykytyn likewise attach a keyed hash (HMAC) and a timestamp to each broadcast telemetry message
of a swarm~\cite{rotta2024telemetrybroadcast}. Vehicular broadcast employs public-key
signatures: ECDSA over each message, with the certificate of the signer in some messages and an
eight-octet digest of it in the others~\cite{etsi_ts103097,ndss2024pqv2v}. We adopt this trust
model, and its per-message cost is the baseline of our evaluation.

\runin{Stream Signing} To amortize one signature over many packets, Gennaro and Rohatgi sign
the first packet and chain the remaining packets by hashes~\cite{gennaro1997streams}; one lost
packet breaks the chain. Wong and Lam sign a hash tree over a block and send in every packet the
hashes needed to reach the root~\cite{wong1999flows}: each packet verifies alone, and the
signing cost is amortized but the bytes are not. EMSS tolerates loss by carrying several hashes
per packet and a periodic signature packet, and TESLA replaces the signature by a Message
Authentication Code (MAC) whose key is disclosed
later~\cite{perrig2000emss,perrig2002tesla}; TBRD applies TESLA to UAS Remote ID and reports
about half the overhead of signatures~\cite{veara2025tbrd}. Erasure codes spread the hashes and
the signature of a block over its packets so that the block verifies when enough of them
arrive, for 10\,B per packet in blocks of sixteen at 5\% loss and a latency of one
block~\cite{pannetrat2003multicast}. However, these schemes spread \emph{one authenticator
across packets}, so packets depend on each other or on a later one. Our design places
\emph{several records in one packet}, which a small telemetry record allows: each frame stays
self-verifying and the cost moves to latency at the sender. TESLA delays verification at the
receiver instead, and a disclosed MAC key provides no non-repudiation, which a ledger requires.
Section~\ref{sec:clas} places these schemes at our operating point.

\runin{Aggregate and Batch Verification} BLS signatures
aggregate~\cite{bls2001,bgls2003}, and certificateless aggregate signature (CLAS) schemes for
vehicular networks build on such constructions~\cite{li2025clas}. Zhang \emph{et al.} verify
many signatures at once at a roadside unit~\cite{zhang2008batch}, and Rajasekaran \emph{et al.}
batch-authenticate a group of drones in a pairing-based scheme, reporting a communication cost
that grows linearly with the group~\cite{rajasekaran2022batchfanet}. All of these reduce the
\emph{work} of the verifier, and an aggregator may forward one short aggregate in place of many
signatures. However, each message, as its signer sends it, still carries its own signature
material (Sec.~\ref{sec:clas}).

\runin{Hash-Linked Records} Linking each record to the hash of its predecessor to make a
sequence tamper-evident is due to Haber and Stornetta~\cite{haber1991timestamp}; blockchain
applications on UAVs are surveyed in~\cite{mehta2020blockchainuav}. We take the chain as given
and quantify what it costs on air.

\runin{Compact Encoding and Small Payloads} That a signature leaves little room in a small
frame is established: G{\"u}ndo{\u{g}}an \emph{et al.} find 9\,B of application data left
beside an Ed25519 signature in an IEEE 802.15.4 frame~\cite{gundogan2021firmware}, and
post-quantum signatures do not fit one 5G broadcast block~\cite{arxiv2510pqsib1}. For
Remote~ID, a 64-octet signature is paged over 25-octet Bluetooth frames with a parity
page~\cite{rfc9575}: the spreading and recovery that our scope excludes. Automatic Dependent
Surveillance--Broadcast (ADS-B) is the extreme case: its 112-bit messages leave 56 bits for
data, and proposals for public-key authentication distribute one signature over several
messages that receivers buffer~\cite{strohmeier2015adsb}. Compact
encodings~\cite{rfc8949} and delta coding of time series~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} are
established, and our earlier evaluation found canonical CBOR byte-identical across 10,000
trials~\cite{farouk2026encoding}; the frame of this paper is a canonical CBOR map. A lossy link
limits how far a delta chain may run (Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}).

\runin{Channel Models} Broadcast frames are not acknowledged and their contention window never
doubles, so the saturation model of Bianchi for the IEEE 802.11 Distributed Coordination
Function (DCF)~\cite{bianchi2000} does not apply as it stands; we employ the broadcast model of
Ma and Chen~\cite{machen2007,machen2008} and validate it against ns-3~\cite{nsnam}. For LoRa,
Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.} model single-gateway scalability~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora} and Zirak
\emph{et al.} report air-to-air delivery against range on
hardware~\cite{zirak2021swarm}. These models describe the channel alone; our evaluation applies
them to each authentication configuration at its own frame size and frame rate.

\section{System and Threat Model}\label{sec:model}

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=444pt]{block_diagram.png}
\caption{System overview: sender pipeline, the four-record frame with its field sizes (bytes),
shared channel, receiver pipeline, and the five constraints that select the design.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:system} gives an overview. Each UAV codes, chains and signs its records into
frames and broadcasts them on a shared channel; each receiver decodes, verifies and stores the
frames of its neighbors; and five constraints fix the design parameters
(Sec.~\ref{sec:constraints}).

\subsection{Network and Traffic}
$N$ UAVs share one channel and all hear one another: one collision domain, which we call a
neighborhood of $N$ nodes. Each UAV produces $\Lambda$ telemetry records per second and
broadcasts them; there are no acknowledgments and no retransmissions. The primary operating
point is $\Lambda = 50$~records/s with a freshness bound $D_{\max} = 100$\,ms: the rate is the
companion-computer stream rate of PX4~\cite{px4_mavlink_streams}, and the pair satisfies the
direct UAV-to-UAV broadcast requirements of 3GPP~\cite{3gpp_ts22125}. A relaxed point of
20~records/s and 250\,ms is reported for comparison.

\subsection{Telemetry Record}
Each record carries nine fields, all fixed-point integers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{ts} -- timestamp in milliseconds
\item \texttt{lat}, \texttt{lon} -- latitude and longitude in degrees$\times 10^{7}$
\item \texttt{alt} -- altitude in centimeters
\item \texttt{vel\_x}, \texttt{vel\_y}, \texttt{vel\_z} -- velocity components in cm/s
\item \texttt{battery} -- battery level in percent
\item \texttt{mode} -- flight mode (enumeration)
\end{itemize}
In the ledger, each record additionally carries the identifier of its sender, its sequence
number and the hash of its predecessor.

\subsection{Ledger}
Each UAV maintains an append-only chain of its own records,
$\mathit{prev\_hash}_{i} = \mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{rec}_{i-1})$, and stores the verified
records it hears from others. No consensus layer is included; the subject of this paper is
authenticated dissemination. A ledger is audited from the uploads alone: one pass over the frames
of a mission, with the public keys of the senders, finds every equivocation and every frame that
does not verify; a planted equivocation and a planted forgery were both found, with no false
finding (Table~\ref{tab:security}). Keeping the frames costs \auKept\,B per record.

\runin{Adversary} The adversary controls the radio: it reads, injects, replays, modifies,
delays and drops frames, and may operate many radios. It holds no private key of an honest UAV.

\runin{What Is Protected} (i)~\emph{Origin and integrity:} a stored record was produced by the
UAV it names and was not altered, by a public-key signature checked before anything is stored.
(ii)~\emph{Replay:} sequence numbers are strictly increasing per sender; a repeated frame is
refused. (iii)~\emph{Equivocation:} two validly signed records for one sequence number are
detected, and the two frames that carried them are kept. The signature covers a frame, so a
record is evidence only with its frame; a third party checks the pair with the sender's public
key alone. (iv)~\emph{Continuity:} omission or reordering within what a receiver holds is
detected through the chain. (v)~\emph{Bounded-delay freshness:} while GNSS time is held, a
receiver refuses a frame stamped more than $\Delta$ before its own clock reads
(Sec.~\ref{sec:fresh}).

\runin{What Is Not Protected} Telemetry is sent in clear. Jamming is not resisted: loss
appears as the robustness measure $V$ below, not as a security property. Flooding is resisted
only from outside the swarm: an 8\,B tag under a key that all members hold refuses the frame of
an outsider before it is decoded, but a member holding that key can still make a receiver
verify forged frames (Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}). A frame delayed by less than $\Delta$ is
accepted, authentic and in order, and so is a later one once GNSS time has been lost for longer
than the window allows. A
UAV with a valid key can sign false data; only inconsistent falsehood is detectable. Key
distribution, revocation and admission are assumed: receivers hold the public keys of the
senders, and the bytes of delivering certificates are charged separately
(Sec.~\ref{sec:certs}). Senders are not anonymous.

\runin{Formal Analysis} Properties (i)--(iv) and the detection of gaps are proven in the Tamarin
prover~\cite{tamarin} for unboundedly many frames, and (v) is shown to hold with the window and
to fail without it (Table~\ref{tab:security}). The proofs use three models, since one model with
both the chain and the clock did not terminate; canonical encoding is assumed injective.

\subsection{Verifiability Metric}
$V$ is the probability that a record a UAV generates is received, decoded and verified by a
given neighbor. We require $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ and take $\epsilon = 0.05$ throughout.

\subsection{Frame Structure}
A frame is one canonical CBOR map~\cite{rfc8949} with integer keys (Table~\ref{tab:fields},
Fig.~\ref{fig:system});
Appendix~\ref{app:frame} provides the complete layout. The fields \texttt{src} and
\texttt{base\_seq} name the sender and the sequence number of the first record; \texttt{link}
is the hash of the record before it; \texttt{recs} is the record stream and \texttt{auth} one
signature. A record in the stream consists of nine variable-length integers. The first record
of a frame is a \emph{keyframe}, carrying absolute values (\keyLean\,B on average); each later
one is a \emph{delta} from the record before it (\deltaLean\,B, the floor of the format, at the
20 and 50\,ms spacings of our two operating points). Sender and sequence number are not
repeated in the records.

\subsection{Signed Content and Verification}
The signature covers not the record stream but the canonical ledger records, each with its own
\texttt{prev\_hash}. The receiver rebuilds them --- the first \texttt{prev\_hash} is
\texttt{link}, and each later one is the hash of the record just rebuilt --- and only then
checks the signature. A frame that cannot be decoded therefore cannot be verified, and nothing
unverified is stored. Each verified record is offered to the ledger store, which refuses a
sequence number that is not newer, keeps an equivocating pair as evidence, and flags a link
that contradicts a record it holds. The state against which a later frame would be decoded
advances only after a frame has been verified and stored, so a replayed frame cannot
desynchronize a stream. Flipping any single bit of a frame makes verification fail (all
\bitflips{} positions of a four-record frame were tried).

\subsection{Behavior Under Frame Loss}
When a frame is lost, the next one still decodes and verifies, because it starts with a
keyframe. Its \texttt{link} then names a record that this receiver does not hold: the gap shows
in the sequence numbers, and the link is stored unchecked, to be checked by any party that
holds the missing record. A late copy of the lost frame is refused, since sequence numbers must
increase; the gap is not repaired.

\subsection{One Chain Link per Frame}
The ledger still chains every record. Only the first link of a frame travels; the receiver
recomputes the others. Inside a frame, the order and content of records therefore rest on the
frame signature alone, whereas in the first format below every record also carries its own hash
on air. A frame is signed and accepted as a unit, so those hashes were redundant and a receiver
loses no check; however, one mechanism remains where there were two.

\subsection{The First Format}
For comparison we retain the format we built first: text keys, and in every record its own
chain link, sender identifier and sequence number. Its header is \hdrFirst\,B, of which
\keyNamesFirst\,B are key names. That format is implemented and frozen. Its sizes in this paper
are sums of separately measured parts, and its delta-coded frames were never emitted; such
sizes are marked wherever they appear.

\subsection{Design Parameters}
A configuration is defined by four parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Batch size} $b$ -- the number of records in a frame.
\item \emph{Signature placement} -- a signature covers each record (\emph{inline};
\texttt{auth} is then an array of $b$ signatures) or the frame (\emph{batch}).
\item \emph{Signature scheme} -- Ed25519~\cite{ed25519} and ECDSA P-256~\cite{fips186} at
64\,B, or BLS12-381~\cite{bls2001} at 96\,B in the library used.
\item \emph{Reference interval} $r$ -- one frame in $r$ starts with a keyframe, and the others
start with a delta against the previous frame.
\end{itemize}

\section{Constraints}\label{sec:constraints}

\runin{Bytes} With header $H_f$, link field $L$, signature \ba, keyframe $s_k$ and delta
$s_\delta$, a self-contained batch frame costs, per record,
\begin{equation}
B(b) = \frac{H_f + L + \ba + s_k + (b-1)\,s_\delta}{b}.
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
$B(1)$ is our baseline: one record, one signature, one frame. Signing each record of a shared
frame replaces \ba{} by $b\,\ba$. Equation~\eqref{eq:bytes} is exact to within a byte of CBOR
length prefix; for the lean design and its baseline we report the lengths of emitted frames.

\runin{Freshness} The oldest record in a frame waits for the frame to fill. Adding the
channel-access and airtime delay $T_{ch}$ and the verification time $t_v$,
\begin{equation}
D(b) = \frac{b}{\Lambda} + T_{ch} + t_v \;\leq\; D_{\max}.
\label{eq:fresh}
\end{equation}
Fill time dominates. At both operating points $\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$: five records fill in
exactly $D_{\max}$ and leave nothing for the channel, so $b \leq 4$ whatever the encoding or the
scheme. Equation~\eqref{eq:fresh} counts the whole filling period $b/\Lambda$. A record may
describe a state up to one sampling interval old; and with $b/\Lambda$ the bound is on the
\emph{peak age} of information~\cite{yates2021aoi} at a receiver that loses no frame: the delay
of the newest record of a frame plus the interval to the next frame. Counting only the wait
$(b-1)/\Lambda$ would admit $b=5$ and \bprLeanFive\,B per record. The 1500\,B MTU would admit
\mtuRecsFirst{} records in the first format and \mtuRecsLean{} in the lean one.

\runin{Verifiability} If one frame in $r$ is self-contained, frame $j$ of a group verifies
only if it and the $j-1$ frames before it arrive. With independent frame loss $p$,
\begin{equation}
V(r,p) = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-p)^{j},
\label{eq:v}
\end{equation}
which equals $1-p$ at $r=1$ and is smaller for every $r>1$. A target $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon \leq p$ therefore admits $r=1$ only. The same argument forbids splitting a signed unit
over $n$ frames, which verifies with probability $(1-p)^n$.

\runin{Channel} For a configuration --- a frame size and a frame rate per node --- $N_{\max}$
is the largest neighborhood in which the mean delivered fraction of frames is at least
$1-\epsilon$. It is obtained by simulation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). As a conservative companion
we report $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$, the largest $N$ whose offered frame rate does not exceed the
saturation throughput of the broadcast DCF model~\cite{machen2008}.

\runin{Receiver CPU} A receiver verifies the frames of every neighbor. With $k$ signatures per
frame, verification time $t_v$ and hash time $t_h$, one neighbor costs
$(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h$ seconds of CPU per second, and one core serves
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{cpu}} = 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:cpu}
\end{equation}
nodes, itself included.

\runin{Payload} A frame that verifies alone must fit the maximum payload $M$ of the link:
\begin{equation}
M \;\geq\; H_f + L + \ba + s_k .
\label{eq:excl}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Implementation Configuration}
Formats, sender, receiver and ledger store form one Python code base with four record encodings
(JSON, CBOR, MessagePack, delta) and three signature schemes behind common interfaces. All
components use standard libraries in their default configurations, without hand-tuned
optimization:
\begin{itemize}
\item cbor2 v\verCbor: canonical encoding~\cite{rfc8949} of frames and of the ledger records
that a signature covers.
\item cryptography v\verCryptography: Ed25519 and ECDSA P-256 signatures; SHA-256 is that of
the Python standard library.
\item blspy v\verBlspy: BLS12-381 signatures.
\item ns-3.48~\cite{nsnam}: channel simulation for IEEE~802.11a and 802.11p, and with its
LoRaWAN module for the low-rate links.
\item Rust with ed25519-dalek: a compiled receiver and sender, byte-identical to the Python
codec on \rustIdFrames{} frames.
\end{itemize}
Each signature scheme is checked against published test vectors (for Ed25519, those
of~\cite{rfc8032}). Table~\ref{tab:platforms} lists the two platforms; the board runs with the
\texttt{\pfPiGovernor} frequency governor.

\subsection{Telemetry Workload}
Records are produced by a seeded random-walk generator: \seeds{} seeds of 1000 records at 50\,ms
spacing, that of the relaxed point. Header fields are sized for a five-digit sender identifier
one hour into a 50\,Hz flight. Section~\ref{sec:px4} checks the generator against flight logs.

\subsection{Measurement Methodology}
\runin{Timing Instrumentation} Signing, verification, hashing and encoding are timed on the
Raspberry~Pi~4 and reported as medians: Ed25519 sign/verify \tSignEd/\tVerEd\us, ECDSA
\tSignEc/\tVerEc\us, BLS \tSignBls/\tVerBls\,ms, and SHA-256 \tHash\us.

\runin{Energy Measurement} Sender energy is metered with an INA219 on the supply rail, five
repetitions per configuration, idle power subtracted: the first format on that board, the lean
format, interpreted and compiled, on a second board and sensor of the same types.

\runin{Channel Simulation} The channel is simulated in ns-3.48~\cite{nsnam}: IEEE~802.11a at
6\,Mb/s, ad hoc mode, broadcast frames, all nodes in range at equal received power, so there is
no capture and no hidden terminal. Each node sends one frame every $b/\Lambda$ seconds, at an
instant drawn uniformly within each period, so the relative phases of the nodes change from
frame to frame. A strictly periodic source is not used, because it freezes every phase for a
whole run: a run then samples one phase configuration, in which a few nodes may collide in
every period while the others lose nothing. The two sources deliver the same on average ---
over \srcPoints{} points and \srcFreshRuns{} runs the means differ by \srcFreshD{} $\pm$
\srcFreshSe{} --- but the strictly periodic one varies \srcSpreadLo--\srcSpreadHi{} times more
from run to run (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

\runin{Statistical Treatment} A run lasts 20\,s and each point comprises \seeds{} runs.
$N_{\max}$ is the largest simulated $N$ whose mean delivered fraction is at least 0.95, every
smaller simulated $N$ also passing. Its interval is the 2.5--97.5 percentile of that quantity
over 10\,000 bootstrap resamples of the seeds. An interval of a mean over seeds is a
Student-$t$ interval, and an interval of an interpolated crossing an expanded percentile
bootstrap interval~\cite{hesterberg2015bootstrap}: with a known truth and \seeds{} seeds, the
plain percentile interval covered \covPlainLo--\covPlainHi\% of the time and these
\covFixedLo--\covFixedHi\%. The saturation models were validated separately
against saturated ns-3 runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). The experiments of
Table~\ref{tab:prereg} were registered before they ran, and are scored as registered,
misses included (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}).

The main evaluation uses one collision domain of static nodes; Section~\ref{sec:capacity}
repeats it for a swarm spread over a wider area, with hidden stations, and moving. Capacities are
obtained by simulation, and contention was measured on hardware between two radios only
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

LoRaWAN limits the payload of a frame by data rate, and its EU863-870 plan defines twelve data
rates~\cite{lora_rp002}: seven LoRa (DR0--DR6), one FSK (DR7) and four LR-FHSS (DR8--DR11). By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl}, a frame that verifies alone requires a header, a chain link, a signature
and one record that decodes alone. The smallest such frame that the lean format can emit at all
--- sender 0, sequence 0, every field zero --- is
$\exFloorHdr + \linkField + 64 + \exFloorRec = \exFloor$\,B; this frame was emitted, signed and
verified. Header, link and signature alone occupy \exFloorNoRec\,B.

Fig.~\ref{fig:excl} compares that floor with the payload limits, revealing three key findings:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \runin{Signature-Bound Rates} Five data rates (DR0--DR2, DR8, DR10) allow 50 or 51\,B:
the signature alone does not fit.
\item \runin{Format-Bound Rates} Three data rates (DR3, DR9, DR11) allow 115\,B: header, link
and signature fill the payload and no record fits.
\item \runin{Feasible Rates} Four data rates (DR4--DR7) allow 222 or 242\,B and can carry a
frame.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{\exclCountCap{} of twelve data rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame}, in either
format and under either of the payload tables of the standard. This exclusion bound does not
depend on the telemetry, since each rate is excluded against the floor of the format;
\exclFmtCount{} of the \exclCount{} depend on the format (condition~v).

\runin{Scope} The result holds under five conditions. (i)~The limits are the regional
parameters of LoRaWAN; the LoRa PHY itself carries 255\,B~\cite{semtech_sx1276} and is not
excluded. (ii)~Every frame carries a public-key signature. (iii)~There is no loss recovery.
(iv)~$\epsilon \leq p$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} forbids spreading a signature over several
frames; at $p=0.01$ and $\epsilon=0.05$ a signed unit may span five frames and the exclusion no
longer holds as stated. (v)~The frame is the format of Section~\ref{sec:frame}. The
\exclSigCount{} rates of 50 and 51\,B are excluded by the signature alone, whatever the format;
at 115\,B a signature and a 32\,B hash leave \exRoomAfterCrypto\,B, which the header and
framing of this format fill exactly. A hand-packed layout would leave part of them for a
record, less than the \keyLean\,B that our keyframe averages.

\runin{Effect of Each Relaxation} Table~\ref{tab:excl} quantifies what each relaxation buys.
Taking the chain link off the air frees the three 115\,B rates: they are excluded by the link,
not by the signature. The smallest standardized signature, 48\,B
BLS12-381~\cite{cfrg_bls}, fits a 51\,B payload and leaves 3\,B; at 115\,B a frame of zeros
would fit (\exFloorBls\,B) but the smallest frame of our telemetry is \exLoBls\,B, a three-byte
miss that we do not count as an exclusion. A 13\,B symmetric tag, as in MAVLink~2, fits the
115\,B rates, but at 50 or 51\,B header and link already take
\exHdrLinkLo--\exHdrLinkHi\,B: a hash-chained frame does not fit there under any authenticator.

\runin{What the Feasible Rates Carry} At DR5 under the 1\% duty cycle a 242\,B frame holds
\loraRecsLean{} records and sustains \loraRateLean~records/s per node (\loraRecsFirst{} records
and \loraRateFirst{} in the first format). The batch must be sized at the record spacing it
implies: a delta that spans \loraSpacing\,s costs \deltaLeanFar\,B, not the \deltaLean\,B it
costs at 50\,ms. The ns-3 LoRaWAN module carries at most 222\,B, where a frame holds
\loraSimRecs{} records. Simulated with a \loraSimBytes\,B payload every \loraSimPeriod\,s
(\loraSimRate~records/s per node), on one channel with one demodulator and no capture, the
largest neighborhood with mean $V \geq 0.95$ is \loraNmax{} nodes (\seeds{} seeds, 95\%
interval \loraNmaxCi; \loraNmaxRun{} if 95\% of runs must each reach it); the closed-form model
of Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.}~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora}, fitted to hardware interference
measurements and evaluated at the same operating point, gives \loraNmaxExt. Such a neighborhood
carries about \loraAgg~records/s in all, where the 802.11 design of
Section~\ref{sec:results} carries some \wifiAgg. On these links the ledger therefore records
provenance every few seconds; it is not the 802.11 design at a smaller scale.

Delta coding is usually run with a keyframe every $K$ records. With $K=16$ and $b=4$ only one

\begin{enumerate}
\item \runin{Dependent Frames Miss the Target} At the loss rate the system is specified for,
$r=4$ verifies \lossVFour{} of records, not 0.95, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} predicts the measurement
at every $r$. Under bursty loss a dependent design loses less --- a Gilbert process with the

\item \runin{Limited Prize} A cleaner link would admit a longer interval ($r=4$ at $p=0.02$,

measurement: at the $\hwLoss$ we measured between two radios (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}) every
interval in the table meets the target, $r=\lossCleanR$ with $V=\lossCleanV$.

costs \bprLeanDesign\,B per record, and loses only its own records when it is lost.

it meets the target, intervals included, under every one of them. At a looser budget the margin
can be too small: at $\epsilon = \mgMissEps$ and $p = \mgMissP$ the design's interval falls below
the target under bursts of eight frames and under bit errors, though its mean does not.

\item \runin{Capacity Gain} The lean baseline holds \nmaxLeanBase{} nodes at 95\% delivery and
the design \nmaxLeanDesign{} (\ratioLean$\times$); in the first format the values are
\nmaxFirstBase{} and \nmaxFirstDesign{} (\ratioFirst$\times$).
\item \runin{Dominant Step} The larger step is sending a quarter as many frames: row~6, which
shares the frame but not the signature, already reaches \nmaxLeanInline{}
(\stepShareLean$\times$ the baseline), and sharing the signature as well adds
\stepSignLean$\times$. Each frame pays a preamble, an inter-frame space, a backoff and MAC
framing whatever its length --- about \fixedCost\us, \fixedSharePct\% of what a baseline frame
costs the channel --- so removing three frames in four is worth more than their bytes.
\item \runin{Stability Across Operating Points} Table~\ref{tab:capacity} repeats the
comparison at the relaxed operating point. The ratio hardly moves --- \ratioLean$\times$ and
\ratioRelLean$\times$ in the lean format, \ratioFirst$\times$ and \ratioRelFirst$\times$ in the
first --- and depends on the format, so we quote it with the format.
\end{enumerate}
The dominant step is the principle of frame aggregation in IEEE~802.11n, whose aggregates are
addressed to one receiver and exclude broadcast~\cite{skordoulis2008aggregation}; batching at
the application gives a broadcast sender the same effect.

\runin{Per-Run Reading} These values hold the mean over runs to the 0.95 target. Requiring
that 95\% of individual runs reach it gives \nrunLeanBase{} and \nrunLeanDesign{}
(\nrunFirstBase{} and \nrunFirstDesign{} in the first format), at most \nrunGap{} nodes fewer.

\runin{The Capacity Envelope} At the 5\% level, loss follows a line in airtime. With
$f = \Lambda/b$ the frames per second a node sends and $T$ the time one frame holds the medium
(its airtime plus DIFS),
\begin{equation}
N_{\max} \approx \frac{0.05}{f\,(a\,T + c)}, \qquad a = \lineA,\quad c = 8\,\mu\mathrm{s},
\label{eq:line}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is twice the preamble-detection time of the simulator and $a$ is the one fitted
constant. It was fitted to six rows of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8), which the
rule then reproduces within \lineFitWorst\%. Twelve further configurations were predicted from
the rule before they were simulated: seven others of our own, all within \lineWorst\% of it,
and the five one-record frames of Table~\ref{tab:stream}, within \lineStreamWorst\% and all
above it. A single utilization ceiling, measured at one point and applied to all, misses the
first thirteen (six fitted, seven predicted) by up to \lineCeilWorst\%, in both directions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:line} is a fit at one loss level and one PHY rate, checked for frames of
\lineFrameLo--\lineFrameHi\,B, \lineFpsLo--\lineFpsHi~frames/s and \lineNlo--\lineNhi{} nodes;
the tables quote simulated values, not the rule. All of those configurations hold one record or
four. Batches of two, three and eight, which deadlines of 60, 80 and 180\,ms would admit, were
predicted before they were simulated: delivery crosses 0.95 at \batNsTwo, \batNsThree{} and
\batNsEight{} nodes, within \batLineWorst\% of the rule and \batModelWorst\% of the access-rule
model described next. Fig.~\ref{fig:deadline} sets them on the rule: it is what a deadline buys
at this record rate, and at 40\,ms it buys nothing.

\runin{Mechanism of the Crossing} The slope is not a free constant. A frame is lost in this
scenario in two ways. In the first, two stations that found the medium busy draw the same
backoff slot. With occupancy $\rho \approx N f T$ and a contention window of $W = 16$ slots,
counting the stations that wait together gives a loss of about $\rho^2/(W(1-\rho))$, hence
$a = \rho/(W(1-\rho))$, which is \modelSlope{} at $\rho = \modelRho$, the mean occupancy at
which loss reaches 5\%. This mechanism is known from analyses of vehicular safety
broadcast~\cite{ma2009dsrc,cao2021dsrc}. In the second, which those analyses do not contain, a
station decides to send within the 4\us{} in which it cannot yet sense another: that is $c$.
Without the first-order step, the count is $\rho\,[1-(1-1/W)^{\rho/(1-\rho)}]$; with the
detection term and nothing fitted, solving it for 5\% gives the \modelCells{} crossings within
\closedWorst\% (mean \closedMean\%).

An event simulation of the access rule alone, with nothing fitted and none of the code of the
simulator, reproduces all \modelCells{} simulated crossings within \modelWorst\%. For crossings
that had not been simulated --- the contention window doubled, and loss levels of 2\% and 10\%
--- it predicted all \modelPredCases{} within \modelPredWorst\%. Doubling the window moves the
crossing of the baseline only from \modelBaseC{} to \modelNsWindowC{} nodes (predicted
\modelPredWindowC): stations that count down for longer wait together more often. The
capacities therefore follow from the access rule and one detection time, and they are the
capacities of radios that keep that rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\runin{Unequal Received Power and Capture} All of this puts every node at one point: equal
received power, so a frame that overlaps another is lost everywhere. With the nodes placed
uniformly in a disc and power falling as in free space, a receiver keeps the strongest of the
frames that start together if it is 4\,dB above the rest. The access-rule model with that one
rule, read in the source of the simulator, gave \sprModelBase{} and \sprModelDesign{} nodes for
baseline and design before the runs. In a disc of radius 15\,m, where every station still
senses every other by energy, ns-3 gives \sprNearBase{} and \sprNearDesign; at 100\,m, the
scale of a swarm, \sprFarBase{} and \sprFarDesign, at most \sprFarBelowPct\% lower. The
equal-power capacities of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} are therefore conservative for unequal power,
by \sprGainLo--\sprGainHi\%, and the ratio of design to baseline stays \sprRatioNear$\times$.
Both discs keep every pair in range: hidden stations, which would lower the capacity, are not
in this test.

\runin{A Spread, Moving Swarm} Hidden stations appear when a swarm is spread wider than one
sensing range. With nodes in discs of radius 100\,m to 1\,km, log-distance path loss, Nakagami
fading and the same capture rule, and in a grouped and a moving layout, the design keeps its
advantage: the ratio of the crossings lies between \spRatioLo{} and \spRatioHi{} in every
layout (Fig.~\ref{fig:spread}). At a radius of 1\,km, \spHiddenPct\% of the nodes able to spoil a frame cannot hear its
sender. Counted in neighbors within reach, capacity falls, to \spNeighbours{} at 1\,km, because
a receiver contends with every node in its carrier-sensing range~\cite{hu2020hidden}; counted in
nodes over the area, it rises to \spArea. With every node on its own Gauss--Markov path at up to
15\,m/s~\cite{bujari2017fanet}, delivery changes by at most \mvDeltaV{} and the ratio stays
\mvRatioLo--\mvRatioHi; the channel model has no Doppler and no time-correlated fading.

\runin{Choosing the Batch at Run Time} A controller that sets $b$ from the occupancy of the
channel it measures was simulated on a swarm that grows while it flies. As registered, with a
margin of 0.01, it met $V \geq 0.95$ in \adRegShare\% of one-second windows and failed at
\adRegFail{} nodes; with a margin of 0.02 it met it in \adShare\% and failed at \adFail, against
\adFixShare\% and \adFixFail{} with $b=4$ fixed, while saving \adAgeSaved\,ms of peak age
(Fig.~\ref{fig:adaptive}). These ages include the instant at which the simulated source sends
each frame, drawn over the next period (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), and are on average half a frame
period longer than those of Sec.~\ref{sec:fresh}. Choosing $b$ at run time buys freshness at
the cost of reliability margin; it does not raise capacity.

\runin{Receiver CPU} Equation~\eqref{eq:cpu} charges cryptography only. By it, one core of the
Raspberry~Pi~4 serves a neighborhood of \ncpuBatch{} nodes when each frame carries one Ed25519
signature and of \ncpuInline{} when every record is signed. At its $N_{\max}$ the design uses
\cpuLeanDesign\% of a core, so by this count the channel binds first. Row~6 shows that the
count can bind: with a signature on every record it reaches the limit of the channel at
\cpuLeanInline\% of a core. With BLS a verification takes \tVerBls\,ms and one core serves
\ncpuBls{} nodes: on this hardware BLS is excluded by CPU well before the channel is reached,
in addition to its larger signature. Ed25519 was designed for batch
verification~\cite{ed25519}: on the Pi~4 a batch of 64 takes \edBatchRatio{} of the time per
signature. The prototype verifies one signature at a time, and ECDSA does not admit batches.

The receiver we built does more than verify, and for it the order is reversed. Decoding a frame
and rebuilding its records, in an interpreted prototype, bring the time to receive a
four-record frame on the Pi~4 to \leanRecvMs\,ms, \leanRecvOverVerify{} times its verification.
By that time one core serves \ncpuProto{} nodes: the processor binds before the channel. Run as
one receiver process per core, one to four at once, the board serves \mcNodesOne, \mcNodesTwo,
\mcNodesThree{} and \mcNodesFour{} nodes. Four cores give \mcScaleFour{} times one, less than we
had written down beforehand, so \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes need three of them. Compiled in Rust, the
same receive path takes \rxRustMs\,ms on the Pi~4, \rxSpeedup{} times less, and the channel
binds again (Table~\ref{tab:receiver}). A receiver that uses records once their group tag and
frame are checked and verifies their signatures in batches before they enter the ledger serves
\rxLazyNodes{} nodes per core. A record is then evidence within \rxEvidenceMs\,ms by the
convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fresh}; in that window a member holding the group key could have
records acted on that are later purged.

\runin{Flooding} A forged frame --- a real sender, a newer sequence number and a well-formed
signature by another key --- costs the Python receiver \flForgedMs\,ms on the Pi~4, against
\flHonestMs\,ms for an honest one, which also pays for its storage. With \flSenders{} senders on
one receiver core, \flNoneRate{} such frames per second push honest delivery below 0.95. An 8\,B
group tag refuses the frame of an outsider in \flTagUs\us: at 3000 forged frames per second,
more than the \flChannel{} that one channel carries, honest delivery stays at \flOutsiderV. A
member holding the key is stopped neither by the tag nor by a per-sender rate cap: honest
delivery falls below 0.95 from \flInsiderRate{} forged frames per second behind the tag, and from
\flSpreadRate{} behind the cap when the forgeries name every sender (Fig.~\ref{fig:flood}).
Against one forging sender the cap keeps delivery at \flCapOneV{} or more, up to the rate one
channel carries. The tag costs \flTagCostPct\% of capacity.

\subsection{Certificates}\label{sec:certs}
The rows so far assume that receivers already hold the public keys of the senders. Vehicular
practice sends a full certificate in every fifth message and an eight-octet digest in the
others~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}. Charging that to every frame --- a \certBytes\,B certificate, so
\certPerFrame\,B per frame on average --- gives the first ``with cert.'' column of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (baseline: \certLeanBase\,B vs.\ design: \certLeanDesign\,B per record).
That reading favors the design: the practice it copies sends ten messages a second, so one in
five is one certificate in \certIntervalMs\,ms, and a baseline of 50 frames/s charged per frame
sends five times that. The second column charges one certificate per \certIntervalMs\,ms at any
frame rate (baseline: \certTLeanBase\,B vs.\ design: \certTLeanDesign\,B); batching then
amortizes only the digest. These are explicit certificates; implicit ones are shorter (a signed
message of at most 226\,B against 330\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}) and are not priced, so both
columns are upper bounds.

\subsection{Representativeness of the Synthetic Telemetry}\label{sec:px4}
Every size above comes from one generator, whose delta record is exactly \deltaLean\,B in every
sample --- the floor of the format. Twelve public PX4 flight logs (four quadrotors, two
hexa/octorotors, three fixed wings, three VTOLs; \pxRecords{} in-flight records), chosen by a
rule fixed before any was opened, were passed through the same encoder. The logs carry position
at 5\,Hz, so the comparison is at 0.2\,s spacing and above, not at the 50\,Hz of the operating
point.

\runin{Flight Logs} At 0.2\,s a real delta record averages \pxDelta\,B against the
\pxDeltaGen\,B of the generator, and a real keyframe \pxKey\,B against \pxKeyGen. The spread is
by speed: the six multicopters, mostly hovering, average \pxDeltaSlow\,B and the three fixed
wings up to \pxDeltaFast\,B. With real sizes at 0.2\,s the design saves \pxSave\%
(\pxSaveWorst\% in the least favorable log) where the generator gives \pxSaveGen\%. The
generator is representative to within about one percentage point, and optimistic for fast
vehicles.

\runin{Operating Rate} For the operating rate itself we flew PX4 in software-in-the-loop ---
its own estimator, logger and MAVLink module on a simulated quadrotor --- with position logged
every 20\,ms. Over \sitlRecords{} in-flight records, at up to \sitlSpeedMax\,m/s, every delta
record is \deltaLean\,B, the floor of the format; the keyframe averages \sitlKey\,B and the
design costs \sitlBpr\,B per record, \sitlGenPct\% less than with the records of the generator.
The 50\,Hz position stream of the same autopilot holds a \sitlPeriod\,ms period to within
$\pm\sitlJitter$\,ms (central 68\%).

\runin{Richer Records and Other Airframes} \afFlights{} flights of the same
autopilot's simulator, ten each of a quadrotor, a fixed wing and a VTOL, the fixed wings at their planned
\afSpeedLo--\afSpeedHi\,m/s, give records of 9, 15 and 25 fields at the 20\,ms spacing of the
operating point. Almost every field changes by less than its first byte holds, so a delta costs
about a byte per field on every airframe, a fixed wing \afWingExtra\,B more than the quadrotor. With 15
fields, adding attitude and body rates, the design saves \afSaveFifteen\% of the bytes and serves
\afRatioFifteen{} times the nodes; with 25, adding navigation quality, airspeed, wind, mission
state and thrust, \afSaveTwentyFive\% and \afRatioTwentyFive{} times. The gain is set by the
number of frames, not by the size of the record (Table~\ref{tab:airframes}).

\subsection{Validation of the Models}\label{sec:validation}
\runin{Models Against the Simulator} The broadcast model~\cite{machen2008} matches saturated
ns-3 runs within $\pm\valBcast$\% over $N=5$--50 (\seeds{} seeds).

\runin{Access Rule Against Two Radios} On two Raspberry~Pi~4 radios, one transmitter alone
loses \hwLossFrames{} frames in \hwSentFrames{} ($p = \hwLoss$). With both radios saturated,
\hwSatOff\% of the frames put on air are lost where the access rule gives \hwSatStd\%, a
prediction registered before the run. Below saturation the agreement is poorer: as the medium
fills from half to 85\% the radios lose \hwPoolLowRatio, \hwPoolMidRatio{} and
\hwPoolHighRatio{} times what the model predicts, and the registered prediction held at the
first two loads and failed at the third. The cause is not established. Below saturation a radio
often holds two frames of its own, and a radio of this family sends its queued frames closer
together than the standard allows --- one per \hwCycleOn\,ms against \hwCycleStd\,ms --- as
commercial cards are known to~\cite{bianchi2007cards}. That state is common with two busy
radios and rare at the operating point. However, a follow-up that switched off the frame
bursting of the vendor, with four further predictions registered, failed all four: bursting has
no demonstrated effect on the loss. No capacity was measured: the capacities in this paper rest
on simulation.

\runin{Stream Signing} Table~\ref{tab:stream} places the schemes of
Section~\ref{sec:related} at our operating point. Each was implemented and run in ns-3 in the
lean one-record frame, with its own authenticator in place of the signature. All of them send a
frame per record --- \strFpsSig~frames/s per node (\strFpsEmss{} for EMSS, with its
signature packets) where the design sends \strFpsDesign{} --- so the fixed channel time of a
frame is paid for every record whatever the size of the authenticator. A 13\,B tag in place of
a 64\,B signature raises $N_{\max}$ from \strNmaxSig{} to \strNmaxMav{} nodes and gives up
non-repudiation; four records in a frame raise it to \strNmaxDesign. Counting a record only
when it verifies within the 100\,ms deadline, only MAVLink~2, Wong--Lam and TESLA with a short
disclosure delay reach 95\%; Gennaro--Rohatgi and every variant of EMSS never do, because a
record waits for packets sent after it. Tree chaining is the one scheme that keeps what a ledger requires --- every packet
verifies alone and its origin can be shown to a third party --- and it pays \strAuthWl\,B per
packet for it. These schemes amortize the cost of signing; on a contended channel the cost that
binds is the frame.

\runin{Aggregate Signatures} The nearest published comparators are certificateless aggregate
signature schemes for vehicular broadcast. Table~\ref{tab:clas} lists what one signer sends per
message. For $n$ messages that is $n$ times as much, since each carries its own identity, key
and signature components. Aggregation acts afterwards, at a roadside unit: it reduces the work
of the verifier and what the unit forwards --- in the one scheme read at source, 388\,B for a
single signature and 784\,B for the aggregate of a hundred~\cite{wang2022clas}. Batching acts
before, on the own records of a sender. The two act on different links, and the table should
not be read as a ranking: those schemes provide conditional privacy, which AUTHBC does not;
their group elements are 128\,B where our signature is 64\,B; and their values are for a
different message. What the comparison does show is that per-message authentication of this
kind costs several hundred bytes, and that aggregation alone does not change that.

\subsection{Interpretation of Key Findings}
The \ratioLean$\times$ capacity gain of the lean design stems from the fixed channel cost of a
frame and only secondarily from its payload: a frame costs about \fixedCost\us{} whatever its
length, so a node that sends \strFpsDesign~frames/s where per-record signing sends \strFpsSig{}
removes three of every four such costs. Sharing the frame alone therefore yields
\stepShareLean$\times$, and sharing the signature a further \stepSignLean$\times$.

The batch of four is a consequence of the freshness bound and not of the MTU: the fill time
$b/\Lambda$ dominates Eq.~\eqref{eq:fresh}, whereas the MTU would admit \mtuRecsLean{} records.
The keyframe in every frame is a consequence of the verifiability target: with a target equal
to the loss rate, Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} admits $r=1$ only, and the most a longer interval could save
is \lossPrize\,B per record.

The neutrality of the signature scheme reflects that Ed25519 and ECDSA produce frames of one
size and differ in verification time only, which enters the CPU constraint and not the bytes.
The exclusion on LoRaWAN has two distinct causes: \exclSigCount{} data rates cannot hold a
64\,B signature in any format, and \exclFmtCount{} cannot hold the header of this format beside
a signature and a chain link.

The same accounting applies to other broadcast systems. ADS-B 1090ES and Bluetooth~4 Remote~ID
cannot carry a signature at all. Bluetooth~5 and Wi-Fi Remote~ID fit up to \olRidRecords{}
records in a frame, but at one message a second no frame meets any deadline up to 1\,s. V2X
CAM at 10\,Hz admits no frame at 100\,ms, \olCamTwo{} record at 200\,ms and \olCamThree{} at
300\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:links}). This is consistent with these standards signing each message; it
does not explain why they do.

\subsection{Deployment Recommendations}
Our findings enable the following configuration guidance; the capacities behind it are
simulated. (1)~\emph{High-rate 802.11 swarms} ($\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$): one Ed25519 signature
and one chain link per four records with a keyframe in every frame, at \bprLeanDesign\,B per
record (per-record signing: \bprLeanBase\,B). (2)~\emph{Tight deadlines}
($\Lambda D_{\max} \leq 2$): per-record signing, since no batch meets the deadline.
(3)~\emph{LoRaWAN provenance links}: the four feasible data rates only, with the batch sized at
the record spacing it implies; on the other \exclCount{} rates a signed, chained frame that
verifies alone cannot be carried, and Table~\ref{tab:excl} lists what each relaxation buys.
(4)~\emph{Constrained receivers}: the processor is provisioned before the channel, since the
interpreted receiver serves \ncpuProto{} nodes per core, a compiled one \rxRustNodes, and BLS
\ncpuBls. (5)~\emph{Loss budget}: $\epsilon$ set at least 0.02 above the expected frame loss,
since at $\epsilon = p$ the design meets its target with no margin (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}). (6)~\emph{Outsiders}: an
8\,B group tag, at \flTagCostPct\% of capacity. These recommendations are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:decision}.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\runin{Capacities Are Simulated} On hardware, contention was measured between two radios only
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). The capacities in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} come from ns-3 with one
collision domain, equal received power and static nodes. Unequal power with capture was
simulated separately and raises them (Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}); a swarm spread over a wider area,
with hidden stations, and a moving swarm were simulated as well, with no Doppler and no
time-correlated fading. Send instants are
redrawn within each period (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). The own stream of PX4, in simulation, holds
its period to about $\pm\sitlJitter$\,ms, which is nearer to the strictly periodic source and
its wider spread between runs.

\runin{Telemetry Is Synthetic} Record sizes come from a generator. The check against flight
logs covers twelve logs of one autopilot at 5\,Hz position resolution, and simulated flights of
that autopilot at 50\,Hz, whose navigation-quality fields do not vary.

\runin{Loss Models} The loss experiment uses independent, Gilbert and length-dependent loss
generated at the frame level, and collision loss traced in ns-3; no loss was measured on air.
Under length-dependent loss the design is below its target where the frame of the baseline
loses 5\% (Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}). Batches of two, three and eight were simulated for the
capacity rule alone, and nothing else here is evaluated at them.

\runin{Energy and CPU} Only the sender is metered, with a sensor never checked against a
reference load; receiver and radio energy are modeled. The CPU share of the receiver counts
verification and hashing, not decoding; counting both, the interpreted prototype needs three of
the four cores of the board for the neighborhood of the design. The compiled receiver was timed
alone, without a radio or a dispatcher in the path.

\runin{Security Scope} Confidentiality, availability and key management are outside the design
(Sec.~\ref{sec:model}). A swarm member holding the group key can still flood, and a delayed
frame is refused only while GNSS time is held. The proofs use three models, not one, and assume
canonical encoding injective. With one link per frame, continuity inside a frame rests on the
frame signature alone, and showing one record to a third party means showing its frame. The
receiver therefore keeps every accepted frame, at its on-air length for every $b$ records held.

\runin{Post-Quantum Signatures} Falcon-512 fits a frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}). An ML-DSA
signature is 2420\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v} and exceeds the 1500\,B MTU with any header, so it
cannot ride in one frame at any batch size; freshness caps the batch at four, so it could not be
amortized either. Such a signature would need a design built around
fragmentation~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v,sobati2025fragmentsec}.

\runin{Multi-Hop} Relayed records are not evaluated. A relay cannot re-sign: it carries the
signatures of the originators or one BLS aggregate~\cite{bgls2003}. Both are implemented;
neither is measured here.

\section{Conclusion}
Authenticated UAV telemetry on a hash-chained ledger imposes a cost that we did not find
accounted for jointly in prior work: a signature and a chain link larger than the record they
protect, on a channel where bytes are airtime. This work addresses that gap by costing
authentication at the frame and not at the record, through emitted frames, ns-3 simulation
with \seeds{} seeds per point, and metered sender energy; what a frame must carry decides where
it can be sent.

Two feasibility boundaries result. A frame that verifies alone needs a header, a chain link, a
signature and one self-contained record: \exclCount{} of the twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN data
rates cannot carry that at any batch size, \exclSigCount{} because the signature alone does not
fit and \exclFmtCount{} because of the own header of this frame. Where the frame fits, one
signature and one chain link per four records cut the bytes per record by \saveLean\%
(\bprLeanBase\,B vs.\ \bprLeanDesign\,B) and let one 802.11a collision domain carry
\nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes where per-record signing carries \nmaxLeanBase. Two constraints shape
the design: the batch is capped by freshness, not by the MTU, and a frame that depends on its
predecessor does not meet a verifiability target equal to the loss rate. Batching and header
design decide what can run; among 64\,B signatures the scheme does not. The capacity values
are simulated, also for a spread and moving swarm, where the gain stays
\spRatioLo--\mvRatioHi-fold; on hardware the access rule behind them held between two saturated
radios and not at every load below saturation, and no capacity was measured. Compiled, the
receiver serves \rxRustNodes{} nodes on one core of a Raspberry~Pi~4, and the lean sender spends
\enRustMeas\,$\mu$J per record, a third of what the first format spends.

This accounting --- what a frame that verifies alone must carry, and which constraint binds
--- is not specific to FANETs and should carry over to other broadcast systems whose records
are small beside their authenticator; this paper evaluates it for UAV telemetry only. Future
work should address the 5G NR sidelink, the 3GPP link for direct broadcast between vehicles, and
a variant for post-quantum signatures that do not fit one frame.

\section{AUTHBC Frame Layout}\label{app:frame}
For reproducibility, the complete layout of the lean frame is provided. A frame is one
canonical CBOR map with the integer keys 0--7:
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
frame = {
  0: 2,           ; v: format version
  1: 0 / 1 / 2,   ; t: frame type
  2: uint,        ; src: sender id (16 bit)
  3: uint,        ; base_seq (32 bit)
  4: 1..255,      ; n: records in frame
  5: bytes(32),   ; link: prev_hash of rec 0
  6: bytes,       ; recs: record stream
  7: bytes(64)    ; auth: one signature, or
     / [n * bytes(64)]     ; one per record
}
rec = 9 varints: ts lat lon alt
      vel_x vel_y vel_z battery mode
\end{verbatim}
}
\runin{Key Implementation Notes}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Frame Types:} Type 0 carries one signature per record; type 1 carries one
signature and decodes alone; type 2 carries one signature and codes its first record against
the last record of the previous frame.
\item \emph{Record Coding:} A keyframe record carries absolute values, with \texttt{ts},
\texttt{battery} and \texttt{mode} unsigned and the other fields zig-zag signed; a delta record
carries zig-zag differences from the record before it. All values are LEB128 variable-length
integers.
\item \emph{Derived Fields:} The sender identifier and sequence number of record $i$ are
\texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq}$\,+\,i$. Every \texttt{prev\_hash} after the first is
recomputed by the receiver and never read from the frame.
\item \emph{Signed Bytes:} A signature covers the canonical CBOR encoding of the rebuilt
ledger records, not the record stream.
\end{itemize}
