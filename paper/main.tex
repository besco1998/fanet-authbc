\begin{abstract}
A UAV that broadcasts signed, hash-chained telemetry spends more bytes on authentication than on
data: a 64\,B signature and a 32\,B chain link accompany a \keyLean\,B record. AUTHBC
makes the frame the unit of design: a format, a sender and a receiver in which one signature and
one chain link cover $b$ records and every frame decodes and verifies alone. Batch size, encoding
and signature scheme are chosen together under freshness, verifiability, channel capacity and
receiver CPU; batching gains nothing unless record rate times deadline exceeds two. Where the
frame does not fit: when every frame must verify alone, \exclCount{} of twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN
data rates cannot carry one (\exclSigCount{} cannot hold the signature in any format,
\exclFmtCount{} our frame). Where it does: at
50~records/s and a 100\,ms deadline the design sends \bprLeanDesign\,B per record against
\bprLeanBase\,B for per-record signing, and in ns-3 one 802.11a collision domain delivers 95\% of
frames among \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes instead of \nmaxLeanBase. Sender energy is metered.
Capacities are simulated; contention was measured on two radios. Record sizes are synthetic,
checked against twelve flight logs and a simulated autopilot.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Unmanned aircraft in a swarm tell each other where they are many times a second. If those
reports are to be evidence afterwards --- of who was where, and of what each vehicle claimed ---
each one must be attributable to its sender and the sequence must be tamper-evident. The textbook
construction signs every record and links it to its predecessor by a hash. The textbook cost is
that the protection is larger than the thing protected: an Ed25519 or ECDSA signature is 64\,B and
a SHA-256 link is 32\,B, while a compactly encoded position, velocity and status record is about
\keyLean\,B. On a shared radio channel those bytes are airtime, and airtime decides how many
neighbours can be heard.

Practice today signs per message. MAVLink~2 appends a 13\,B symmetric tag to each
message~\cite{mavlink2signing}; vehicular standards attach an ECDSA signature, and periodically a
certificate, to each one~\cite{etsi_ts103097}. The obvious alternative is to let several records
share one frame, one signature and one chain link. Batching is not new, and its price is known in
kind: records wait for the frame to fill, and a lost frame loses all of them. What we did not
find is the accounting. What must a frame carry so that a receiver can verify it without any
other frame? On which links does such a frame not fit, whatever the batch? Where it fits, which
constraint caps the batch, and what do the saved bytes buy on a contended channel, in nodes
and not in percent? And can that be known without simulating every configuration?

We answer these for one concrete system. AUTHBC --- authenticated telemetry for a
blockchain-style, hash-chained ledger --- is a frame format, a sender and a receiver. We built it,
measured it, and searched its parameters. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A capacity rule with its mechanism.} Capacity is found by simulating each
configuration at its own frame size and rate, with \seeds{} seeds and a bootstrap interval, not
by applying one load ceiling to all. A rule with one fitted constant, the capacity envelope of
the title, reproduces the simulated capacities. The access rule accounts for the constant and,
with one loss mechanism that earlier analyses of broadcast do not contain, gives the capacities
with nothing fitted. Every prediction made from either was written down before its runs, other
batch sizes and unequal received power among them (Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}).
\item \textbf{A ladder that separates causes.} Sharing a frame, sharing a signature and delta
coding are separate steps, and we report bytes, capacity and receiver CPU for each
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), and sender energy with and without batching. Most of the capacity
is gained by sending fewer frames, the effect frame aggregation exists for.
\item \textbf{An exclusion bound with its scope.} On LoRaWAN, \exclCount{} of twelve EU863-870 data
rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame: \exclSigCount{} because the signature alone does
not fit, \exclFmtCount{} because of this frame's header. We state the five conditions under which
that holds and what each relaxation buys (Sec.~\ref{sec:lowrate}).
\item \textbf{A frame as one object, and which constraint binds.} The design and its baseline
are frames that were emitted, decoded and verified, and their sizes are measured lengths
(Sec.~\ref{sec:frame}); the lean format is \leanVsFirstPct\% smaller than the one we built
first. Freshness, not the MTU, caps the batch at four records. A delta-coded frame that depends
on its predecessor misses the verifiability target at its own loss budget, so every frame
carries one self-contained record. Counting cryptography alone, one CPU core serves a
neighbourhood of \ncpuBatch{} nodes with Ed25519 and of \ncpuBls{} with BLS; the interpreted
receiver we built serves \ncpuProto{} on one core and needs three of the board's four for the
design's neighbourhood.
\end{itemize}

In short, batching and header design decide what can run, and among standard 64\,B
elliptic-curve signatures the choice of scheme does not. AUTHBC introduces no new cryptographic
primitive. What is measured, what is simulated and what is only modelled is stated with each
result and collected in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Per-message authentication.} MAVLink~2 signing appends a link identifier, a 48-bit
timestamp and a 48-bit truncated SHA-256 tag computed with a shared secret~\cite{mavlink2signing}.
It authenticates a \emph{link}: any holder of the key can produce any message, so a record cannot
be attributed to one vehicle afterwards. Rotta and Mykytyn likewise attach an HMAC and a
timestamp to each broadcast telemetry message of a swarm~\cite{rotta2024telemetrybroadcast}.
Vehicular broadcast uses public-key signatures: ECDSA over each message, with the signer's
certificate in some messages and an eight-octet digest of it in the
others~\cite{etsi_ts103097,ndss2024pqv2v}. That is the trust model we adopt, and the per-message
cost we start from.

\textbf{Signing streams.} To amortise one signature over many packets, Gennaro and Rohatgi sign
the first packet and chain the rest by hashes~\cite{gennaro1997streams}; one lost packet breaks
the chain. Wong and Lam sign a hash tree over a block and send in every
packet the hashes needed to reach the root~\cite{wong1999flows}: each packet verifies alone, and
the signing cost is amortised but the bytes are not. EMSS tolerates loss by carrying several
hashes per packet and a periodic signature packet, and TESLA replaces the signature by a MAC
whose key is disclosed later~\cite{perrig2000emss,perrig2002tesla}; TBRD applies TESLA to UAS
Remote ID and reports about half the overhead of signatures~\cite{veara2025tbrd}. Erasure codes
spread a block's hashes and signature over its packets so that the block verifies when enough
of them arrive, for 10\,B per packet in blocks of sixteen at 5\% loss and a latency of one
block~\cite{pannetrat2003multicast}. These schemes spread \emph{one authenticator across
packets}, so packets depend on each other or on a later one. We put \emph{several records in one packet}, which a small telemetry record allows: each
frame stays self-verifying and the cost moves to latency, at the sender. TESLA delays
verification at the receiver instead, and a disclosed MAC key gives no non-repudiation, which a
ledger needs. Section~\ref{sec:clas} places these schemes at our operating point.

\textbf{Aggregate and batch verification.} BLS signatures aggregate~\cite{bls2001,bgls2003}, and
certificateless aggregate schemes (CLAS) for vehicular networks build on such
constructions~\cite{li2025clas}. Zhang \emph{et al.} verify many signatures at once at a
roadside unit~\cite{zhang2008batch}, and Rajasekaran \emph{et al.} batch-authenticate a group
of drones in a pairing-based scheme, reporting a communication cost that grows linearly with
the group~\cite{rajasekaran2022batchfanet}. All of these reduce the verifier's \emph{work}, and an
aggregator may forward one short aggregate in place of many signatures; but each message, as
its signer sends it, still carries its own signature material (Sec.~\ref{sec:clas}).

\textbf{Hash-linked records.} Linking each record to the hash of its predecessor to make a
sequence tamper-evident is due to Haber and Stornetta~\cite{haber1991timestamp}; blockchain uses
on UAVs are surveyed in~\cite{mehta2020blockchainuav}. We take the chain as given and ask what
it costs on air.

\textbf{Small payloads.} That a signature leaves little room in a small frame is known:
G{\"u}ndo{\u{g}}an \emph{et al.} find 9\,B of application data left beside an Ed25519 signature in
an 802.15.4 frame~\cite{gundogan2021firmware}, and post-quantum signatures do not fit one 5G
broadcast block~\cite{arxiv2510pqsib1}. For Remote~ID, a 64-octet signature is paged over
25-octet Bluetooth frames with a parity page~\cite{rfc9575}: the spreading and recovery that
our scope excludes. ADS-B is the extreme case: its 112-bit messages leave 56 bits for data, and
proposals for public-key authentication distribute one signature over several messages that
receivers buffer~\cite{strohmeier2015adsb}. Compact encodings~\cite{rfc8949} and delta coding of time
series~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} are established; a lossy link limits how far a delta chain may
run (Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}).

\textbf{Channel models.} Broadcast frames are not acknowledged and their contention window
never doubles, so Bianchi's saturation model of the 802.11 DCF~\cite{bianchi2000} does not
apply as it stands; we use the broadcast model of Ma and
Chen~\cite{machen2007,machen2008} and validate it against ns-3~\cite{nsnam}. For LoRa,
Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.} model single-gateway scalability~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora} and Zirak
\emph{et al.} report air-to-air delivery against range on hardware~\cite{zirak2021swarm}.

\section{System and Threat Model}\label{sec:model}
\input{fig_system}

\textbf{Network and traffic.} $N$ UAVs share one channel and all hear one another: one collision
domain, which we call a neighbourhood of $N$ nodes. Each produces $\Lambda$ telemetry records per
second and broadcasts them; there are no acknowledgements and no retransmissions. A record holds a millisecond timestamp, latitude,
longitude and altitude, three velocity components, battery level and flight mode, all integers.
Our primary operating point is $\Lambda = 50$~records/s with a freshness bound
$D_{\max} = 100$\,ms: the rate is PX4's companion-computer stream rate~\cite{px4_mavlink_streams}
and the pair meets the direct UAV-to-UAV broadcast requirements of
3GPP~\cite{3gpp_ts22125}. A relaxed point, 20~records/s and 250\,ms, is reported for comparison.

\textbf{Ledger.} Each UAV keeps an append-only chain of its own records,
$\mathit{prev\_hash}_{i} = \mathrm{SHA\mbox{-}256}(\mathit{rec}_{i-1})$, and stores the verified
records it hears from others. There is no consensus layer; the subject of this paper is
authenticated dissemination.

\textbf{Adversary.} The adversary controls the radio: it reads, injects, replays, modifies,
delays and drops frames, and may operate many radios. It holds no honest UAV's private key.

\textbf{What is protected.} (i)~\emph{Origin and integrity:} a stored record was produced by the
UAV it names and was not altered, by a public-key signature checked before anything is stored.
(ii)~\emph{Replay:} sequence numbers are strictly increasing per sender; a repeated frame is
refused. (iii)~\emph{Equivocation:} two validly signed records for one sequence number are detected, and
the two frames that carried them are kept. The signature covers a frame, so a record is
evidence only with its frame; a third party checks the pair with the sender's public key alone.
(iv)~\emph{Continuity:} omission or reordering within what a receiver holds is detected through
the chain.

\textbf{What is not.} Telemetry is sent in clear. Jamming is not resisted: loss appears as the
robustness measure $V$ below, not as a security property. Nor is flooding: every injected frame
that names a known sender costs a receiver one decode and one verification before it is refused. Replay is refused by sequence number,
not by time, so a frame that an attacker withholds and delivers later is accepted, authentic and
in order, however stale; refusing it needs a clock shared with the sender, which we do not assume.
A UAV with a valid key can sign false data; only inconsistent falsehood is detectable. Key
distribution, revocation and admission are assumed: receivers hold senders' public keys, and we
charge the bytes of delivering certificates separately (Sec.~\ref{sec:certs}). Senders are not
anonymous.

\textbf{Verifiability.} $V$ is the probability that a record a UAV generates is received,
decoded and verified by a given neighbour. We require $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ and take
$\epsilon = 0.05$ throughout.

A frame is one canonical CBOR map~\cite{rfc8949} with integer keys (Table~\ref{tab:fields}).
\texttt{src} and \texttt{base\_seq} name the sender and the sequence number of the first record;
\texttt{link} is the hash of the record before it; \texttt{recs} is the record stream and
\texttt{auth} one signature. A record in the stream is nine variable-length integers. The first
record of a frame is a \emph{keyframe}, carrying absolute values (\keyLean\,B on average); each
later one is a \emph{delta} from the record before it (\deltaLean\,B, the floor of the format,
at the 20 and 50\,ms spacings of our two operating points). Sender and sequence number are not
repeated in the records.

\textbf{What is signed} is not the stream but the canonical ledger records, each with its own
\texttt{prev\_hash}. The receiver rebuilds them --- the first \texttt{prev\_hash} is
\texttt{link}, each later one is the hash of the record just rebuilt --- and only then checks the
signature. A frame that cannot be decoded therefore cannot be verified, and nothing unverified is
stored. Each verified record is offered to the ledger store, which refuses a sequence number that
is not newer, keeps an equivocating pair as evidence, and flags a link that contradicts a record
it holds. The state against which a later frame would be decoded advances only after a frame has
been verified and stored, so a replayed frame cannot desynchronise a stream. Flipping any single
bit of a frame makes verification fail (all \bitflips{} positions of a four-record frame were
tried).

\textbf{When a frame is lost,} the next one still decodes and verifies, because it starts with a
keyframe. Its \texttt{link} then names a record this receiver does not hold: the gap shows in the
sequence numbers, and the link is stored unchecked, to be checked by any party that holds the
missing record. A late copy of the lost frame is refused, since sequence numbers must increase;
the gap is not repaired.

\textbf{One link per frame.} The ledger still chains every record. Only the first link of a
frame travels; the receiver recomputes the others. Inside a frame the order and content of records
therefore rest on the frame signature alone, where in the first format below every record also
carries its own hash on air. A frame is signed and accepted as a unit, so those hashes were
redundant and a receiver loses no check; but one mechanism remains where there were two.

\textbf{The first format.} For comparison we keep the format we built first: text keys, and in
every record its own chain link, sender identifier and sequence number. Its header is
\hdrFirst\,B, of which \keyNamesFirst\,B are key names. That format is implemented and frozen.
Its sizes in this paper are sums of separately measured parts, and its delta-coded frames were
never emitted; we mark such sizes wherever they appear.

\textbf{Design parameters.} A configuration is the batch size $b$; whether a signature covers
each record (\emph{inline}; \texttt{auth} is then an array of $b$ signatures) or the frame
(\emph{batch}); the signature scheme (Ed25519~\cite{ed25519} and ECDSA P-256~\cite{fips186} at
64\,B, BLS12-381~\cite{bls2001} at 96\,B in the library used); and the reference interval $r$:
one frame in $r$ starts with a keyframe, the others with a delta against the previous frame.

\section{Constraints}\label{sec:constraints}

\textbf{Bytes.} With header $H_f$, link field $L$, signature \ba, keyframe $s_k$ and delta
$s_\delta$, a self-contained batch frame costs, per record,
\begin{equation}
B(b) = \frac{H_f + L + \ba + s_k + (b-1)\,s_\delta}{b}.
\label{eq:bytes}
\end{equation}
$B(1)$ is our baseline: one record, one signature, one frame. Signing each record of a shared
frame replaces \ba{} by $b\,\ba$. Equation~\eqref{eq:bytes} is exact to within a byte of CBOR length
prefix; for the lean design and its baseline we report the lengths of emitted frames.

\textbf{Freshness.} The oldest record in a frame waits for the frame to fill. Adding the
channel-access and airtime delay $T_{ch}$ and the verification time $t_v$,
\begin{equation}
D(b) = \frac{b}{\Lambda} + T_{ch} + t_v \;\leq\; D_{\max}.
\label{eq:fresh}
\end{equation}
Fill time dominates. At both operating points $\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$: five records fill in exactly
$D_{\max}$ and leave nothing for the channel, so $b \leq 4$ whatever the encoding or the scheme.
Equation~\eqref{eq:fresh} counts the whole filling period $b/\Lambda$. A record may describe a
state up to one sampling interval old; and with $b/\Lambda$ the bound is on the \emph{peak age}
of information~\cite{yates2021aoi} at a receiver that loses no frame: the delay of a frame's
newest record plus the interval to the next frame. Counting only the wait $(b-1)/\Lambda$ would
admit $b=5$ and \bprLeanFive\,B per record. The 1500\,B MTU would admit \mtuRecsFirst{} records in the first format and \mtuRecsLean{} in
the lean one.

\textbf{Verifiability.} If one frame in $r$ is self-contained, frame $j$ of a group verifies only
if it and the $j-1$ frames before it arrive. With independent frame loss $p$,
\begin{equation}
V(r,p) = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(1-p)^{j},
\label{eq:v}
\end{equation}
which equals $1-p$ at $r=1$ and is smaller for every $r>1$. A target $V \geq 1-\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon \leq p$ therefore admits $r=1$ only. The same argument forbids splitting a signed unit
over $n$ frames, which verifies with probability $(1-p)^n$.

\textbf{Channel.} For a configuration --- a frame size and a frame rate per node --- $N_{\max}$
is the largest neighbourhood in which the mean delivered fraction of frames is at least
$1-\epsilon$. We find it by simulation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). As a conservative companion we
give $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$, the largest $N$ whose offered frame rate does not exceed the saturation
throughput of the broadcast DCF model~\cite{machen2008}.

\textbf{Receiver CPU.} A receiver verifies every neighbour's frames. With $k$ signatures per frame,
verification time $t_v$ and hash time $t_h$, one neighbour costs
$(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h$ seconds of CPU per second, and one core serves
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{cpu}} = 1 + \left\lfloor \frac{1}{(\Lambda/b)\,k\,t_v + \Lambda\,t_h} \right\rfloor
\label{eq:cpu}
\end{equation}
nodes, itself included.

\textbf{Payload.} A frame that verifies alone must fit the link's maximum payload $M$:
\begin{equation}
M \;\geq\; H_f + L + \ba + s_k .
\label{eq:excl}
\end{equation}

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\textbf{Implementation.} Formats, sender, receiver and store are one Python code base with four
record encodings (JSON, CBOR, MessagePack, delta) and three signature schemes behind common
interfaces, each scheme checked against published test vectors (for Ed25519,
those of~\cite{rfc8032}).

\textbf{Telemetry.} Records come from a seeded random-walk generator: \seeds{} seeds of 1000
records at 50\,ms spacing, that of the relaxed point. Header fields are sized for a five-digit sender identifier one hour into
a 50\,Hz flight. Section~\ref{sec:px4} checks the generator against flight logs.

\textbf{Timing and energy.} Signing, verification, hashing and encoding are timed on a
Raspberry~Pi~4 (medians; Ed25519 sign/verify \tSignEd/\tVerEd\us, ECDSA \tSignEc/\tVerEc\us, BLS
\tSignBls/\tVerBls\,ms, SHA-256 \tHash\us). Sender energy is metered with an INA219 on the
supply rail, five repetitions per configuration, idle power subtracted: the first format on that
board, the lean format on a second board and sensor of the same types.

\textbf{Channel simulation.} ns-3.48~\cite{nsnam}: IEEE~802.11a at 6\,Mb/s, ad hoc mode,
broadcast frames, all nodes in range at equal received power, so there is no capture and no
hidden terminal. Each node sends one frame every $b/\Lambda$ seconds, at an instant drawn
uniformly within each period, so the relative phases of the nodes change from frame to frame.
We do not use a strictly periodic source, because it freezes every phase for a whole run: a run
then samples one phase configuration, in which a few nodes may collide in every period while the
others lose nothing. The two sources deliver the same on average --- over \srcPoints{} points
and \srcFreshRuns{} runs the means differ by \srcFreshD{} $\pm$ \srcFreshSe{} --- but the
strictly periodic one varies \srcSpreadLo--\srcSpreadHi{} times more from run to run
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

A run lasts 20\,s and each point is \seeds{} runs. $N_{\max}$ is the largest simulated $N$
whose mean delivered fraction is at least 0.95, every smaller simulated $N$ also passing. Its
interval is the 2.5--97.5 percentile of that quantity over 10\,000 bootstrap resamples of the
seeds. The saturation models were validated separately against saturated ns-3 runs
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

LoRaWAN limits the payload of a frame by data rate, and its EU863-870 plan defines twelve data
rates~\cite{lora_rp002}: seven LoRa (DR0--DR6), one FSK (DR7) and four LR-FHSS (DR8--DR11). By Eq.~\eqref{eq:excl} a frame that verifies alone needs a header, a
chain link, a signature and one record that decodes alone. The smallest such frame the lean
format can emit at all --- sender 0, sequence 0, every field zero --- is
$\exFloorHdr + \linkField + 64 + \exFloorRec = \exFloor$\,B, and we emitted, signed and verified
it. Header, link and signature alone are \exFloorNoRec\,B.

Figure~\ref{fig:excl} sets that against the payload limits. Five data rates (DR0--DR2, DR8, DR10)
allow 50 or 51\,B: the signature alone does not fit. Three (DR3, DR9, DR11) allow 115\,B: header,
link and signature fill the payload and no record fits. Four (DR4--DR7) allow 222 or 242\,B and
are feasible. \textbf{\exclCountCap{} of twelve data rates cannot carry one signed, chained frame}, in
either format and under either of the standard's payload tables. None of the \exclCount{} depends
on the telemetry, since each is excluded against the format's floor; \exclFmtCount{} of them depend
on the format (condition~v).

\textbf{Scope.} The result holds under five conditions. (i)~The limits are LoRaWAN's regional
parameters; the LoRa PHY itself carries 255\,B~\cite{semtech_sx1276} and is not excluded.
(ii)~Every frame carries a public-key signature. (iii)~There is no loss recovery. (iv)~$\epsilon
\leq p$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:v} forbids spreading a signature over several frames; at
$p=0.01$ and $\epsilon=0.05$ a signed unit may span five frames and the exclusion no longer holds
as stated. (v)~The frame is the format of Sec.~\ref{sec:frame}. The \exclSigCount{} rates of 50
and 51\,B are excluded by the signature alone, whatever the format; at 115\,B a signature and a
32\,B hash leave \exRoomAfterCrypto\,B, which this format's header and framing fill exactly. A
hand-packed layout would leave part of them for a record, less than the \keyLean\,B our keyframe
averages.

\textbf{What each relaxation buys} (Table~\ref{tab:excl}). Taking the chain link off the air
frees the three 115\,B rates: they are excluded by the link, not by the signature. The smallest
standardised signature, 48\,B BLS12-381~\cite{cfrg_bls}, fits a 51\,B payload and leaves 3\,B; at
115\,B a frame of zeros would fit (\exFloorBls\,B) but the smallest frame of our telemetry is
\exLoBls\,B, a three-byte miss that we do not count as an exclusion. A 13\,B symmetric tag, as in
MAVLink~2, fits the 115\,B rates, but at 50 or 51\,B header and link already take
\exHdrLinkLo--\exHdrLinkHi\,B: a hash-chained frame does not fit there under any authenticator.

\textbf{What the feasible rates carry.} At DR5 under the 1\% duty cycle a 242\,B frame holds
\loraRecsLean{} records and sustains \loraRateLean~records/s per node (\loraRecsFirst{} records
and \loraRateFirst{} in the first format). The batch must be sized at the record spacing it
implies: a delta that spans \loraSpacing\,s costs \deltaLeanFar\,B, not the \deltaLean\,B it
costs at 50\,ms. The ns-3 LoRaWAN module carries at most 222\,B, where a frame holds
\loraSimRecs{} records. Simulated with a \loraSimBytes\,B payload every \loraSimPeriod\,s
(\loraSimRate~records/s per node), on one channel with one demodulator and no capture, the
largest neighbourhood with mean $V \geq 0.95$ is \loraNmax{} nodes (\seeds{} seeds, 95\%
interval \loraNmaxCi; \loraNmaxRun{} if 95\% of runs must each reach it); the closed-form
model of Haxhibeqiri \emph{et al.}~\cite{haxhibeqiri2017lora}, fitted to hardware interference
measurements and evaluated at the same operating point, gives \loraNmaxExt. Such a neighbourhood
carries about \loraAgg~records/s in all, where the 802.11 design of
Section~\ref{sec:results} carries some \wifiAgg. On these links the ledger records provenance
every few seconds; it is not the 802.11 design at a smaller scale.

Delta coding is usually run with a keyframe every $K$ records. With $K=16$ and $b=4$ only one

Table~\ref{tab:loss} measures this with the codec in the loop --- \lossFrames{} frames per

system is specified for, $r=4$ verifies \lossVFour{} of records, not 0.95. Equation~\eqref{eq:v} predicts

process with the same mean and bursts of four frames gives \lossVFourBurst{} at $r=4$ --- but it
does not reach $1-p$. A cleaner link would admit a longer interval ($r=4$ at $p=0.02$, $r=8$ at

specification, not a measurement: at the $\hwLoss$ we measured between two radios
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}) every interval in the table meets the target, $r=\lossCleanR$ with
$V=\lossCleanV$. The design is sized for the budget and therefore uses $r=1$:
each frame starts with a keyframe, costs \bprLeanDesign\,B per record, and loses only its own
records when it is lost.

These figures give every frame the same loss probability. At a fixed bit error rate a longer
frame is lost more often, and a batch is a longer frame. On a channel that loses 5\% of the
baseline's one-record frames, the design's frame is lost with probability \lossBerP:
\lossBerTh{} of its records are expected to verify, below the target, and the measurement,
\lossBerV~\lossBerCi, agrees with that expectation. The design reaches 0.95 only while
one-record frames are lost at most \lossBerPmax{} of the time. The capacities of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} are simulated at equal, strong received power, where frames are lost by
collision and not by bit error, and carry that qualification.

\textbf{The capacity envelope: a rule for the crossing.} At the 5\% level, loss follows a
line in airtime. With
$f = \Lambda/b$ the frames per second a node sends and $T$ the time one frame holds the medium
(its airtime plus DIFS),
\begin{equation}
N_{\max} \approx \frac{0.05}{f\,(a\,T + c)}, \qquad a = \lineA,\quad c = 8\,\mu\mathrm{s},
\label{eq:line}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is twice the simulator's preamble-detection time and $a$ is the one fitted constant. It
was fitted to six rows of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8), which the rule then
reproduces within \lineFitWorst\%. Twelve further configurations were predicted from the rule
before they were simulated: seven others of our own, all within \lineWorst\% of it, and the five
one-record frames of Table~\ref{tab:stream}, within \lineStreamWorst\% and all above it. A
single utilisation ceiling, measured at one point and applied to all, misses the first thirteen
(six fitted, seven predicted) by up to \lineCeilWorst\%, in both directions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:line} is a fit at one loss level and one PHY rate, checked for frames of
\lineFrameLo--\lineFrameHi\,B, \lineFpsLo--\lineFpsHi~frames/s and \lineNlo--\lineNhi{} nodes;
the tables quote simulated values, not the rule. All of those configurations hold one record or
four. Batches of two, three and eight, which deadlines of 60, 80 and 180\,ms would admit, were
predicted before they were simulated: delivery crosses 0.95 at \batNsTwo, \batNsThree{} and
\batNsEight{} nodes, within \batLineWorst\% of the rule and \batModelWorst\% of the access-rule
model described next. Figure~\ref{fig:deadline} sets them on the rule: it is what a deadline
buys at this record rate, and at 40\,ms it buys nothing.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_capacity_vs_deadline.png}
\caption{Nodes the lean design serves at 50~records/s, by batch size and the deadline that admits
it: simulated crossings of 0.95 delivery, and the rule.}
\label{fig:deadline}

\textbf{Why the crossing is there.} The slope is not a free constant. A frame is lost in this
scenario in two ways. In the first, two stations that found the medium busy draw the same
backoff slot. With occupancy $\rho \approx N f T$ and a contention window of $W = 16$ slots,
counting the stations that wait together gives a loss of about $\rho^2/(W(1-\rho))$, hence
$a = \rho/(W(1-\rho))$, which is \modelSlope{} at $\rho = \modelRho$, the mean occupancy at
which loss reaches 5\%. This mechanism is known from analyses of vehicular safety
broadcast~\cite{ma2009dsrc,cao2021dsrc}. In the second, which those analyses do not contain, a
station decides to send within the 4\us{} in which it cannot yet sense another: that is $c$.
Without the first-order step, the count is $\rho\,[1-(1-1/W)^{\rho/(1-\rho)}]$; with the
detection term and nothing fitted, solving it for 5\% gives the \modelCells{} crossings within
\closedWorst\% (mean \closedMean\%).

An event simulation of the access rule alone, with nothing fitted and none of the simulator's
code, reproduces all \modelCells{} simulated crossings within \modelWorst\%. For crossings that
had not been simulated --- the contention window doubled, and loss levels of 2\% and 10\% --- it
predicted all \modelPredCases{} within \modelPredWorst\%. Doubling the window moves the
baseline's crossing only from \modelBaseC{} to \modelNsWindowC{} nodes (predicted
\modelPredWindowC): stations that count down for longer wait together more often. The
capacities therefore follow from the access rule and one detection time, and they are the
capacities of radios that keep that rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).

\textbf{Nodes spread out, and capture.} All of this puts every node at one point: equal
received power, so a frame that overlaps another is lost everywhere. With the nodes placed
uniformly in a disc and power falling as in free space, a receiver keeps the strongest of the
frames that start together if it is 4\,dB above the rest. The access-rule model with that one
rule, read in the simulator's source, gave \sprModelBase{} and \sprModelDesign{} nodes for
baseline and design before the runs. In a disc of radius 15\,m, where every station still
senses every other by energy, ns-3 gives \sprNearBase{} and \sprNearDesign; at 100\,m, a
swarm's scale, \sprFarBase{} and \sprFarDesign, at most \sprFarBelowPct\% lower. The
equal-power capacities of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} are therefore conservative for unequal power,
by \sprGainLo--\sprGainHi\%, and the ratio of design to baseline stays \sprRatioNear$\times$.
Both discs keep every pair in range: hidden stations, which would lower the capacity, are not
in this test.


\textbf{Receiver CPU.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cpu} charges cryptography only. By it, one core of the
Raspberry~Pi~4 serves a neighbourhood of \ncpuBatch{} nodes when each frame carries one Ed25519
signature and of \ncpuInline{} when every record is signed. At its $N_{\max}$ the design uses
\cpuLeanDesign\% of a core, so by this count the channel binds first. Row~6 shows that the count
can bind: with a signature on every record it reaches the channel's limit at \cpuLeanInline\% of
a core. With BLS a verification takes \tVerBls\,ms and one core serves \ncpuBls{} nodes: on this
hardware BLS is excluded by CPU well before the channel is reached, in addition to its larger
signature. Ed25519 was designed for batch verification~\cite{ed25519}: on the Pi~4 a batch of
64 takes \edBatchRatio{} of the time per signature. The prototype verifies one signature at a
time, and ECDSA does not admit batches.

The receiver we built does more than verify, and for it the order is reversed. Decoding a frame
and rebuilding its records, in an interpreted prototype, bring the time to receive a four-record
frame on the Pi~4 to \leanRecvMs\,ms, \leanRecvOverVerify{} times its verification. By that time
one core serves \ncpuProto{} nodes: the processor binds before the channel. Run as one receiver
process per core, one to four at once, the board serves \mcNodesOne, \mcNodesTwo,
\mcNodesThree{} and \mcNodesFour{} nodes. Four cores give \mcScaleFour{} times one, less than we
had written down beforehand, so \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes need three of them. The figures of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpu} are ceilings for a compiled receiver.

\textbf{Other PHY rates.} We simulated one rate. For the others we give only the saturation
bound, which at 6\,Mb/s rises from \nsatLeanBase{} to \nsatLeanDesign{} nodes. With the same
model at 12 and 24\,Mb/s, and on the 10\,MHz channel of 802.11p at 3--12\,Mb/s, the
design-to-baseline ratio of $N_{\mathrm{sat}}$ runs from \phyRatioLo$\times$ to
\phyRatioHi$\times$ over both formats, growing with the rate: a faster PHY shortens the payload
but not the fixed cost of a frame.

\subsection{Certificates}\label{sec:certs}
The rows so far assume that receivers already hold the senders' public keys. Vehicular practice
sends a full certificate in every fifth message and an eight-octet digest in the
others~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}. Charging that to every frame --- a \certBytes\,B certificate, so
\certPerFrame\,B per frame on average --- gives the first ``with cert.'' column of
Table~\ref{tab:ladder}: \certLeanDesign\,B per record for the design against \certLeanBase{} for
the baseline. That reading favours the design: the practice it copies sends ten messages a
second, so one in five is one certificate in \certIntervalMs\,ms, and a baseline of 50 frames/s
charged per frame sends five times that. The second column charges one certificate per
\certIntervalMs\,ms at any frame rate, \certTLeanDesign\,B against \certTLeanBase; batching
then amortises only the digest. These are explicit certificates; implicit ones are shorter (a signed message of at
most 226\,B against 330\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v}) and are not priced, so both columns are upper
bounds.

\subsection{Is the synthetic telemetry representative?}\label{sec:px4}
Every size above comes from one generator, whose delta record is exactly \deltaLean\,B in every
sample --- the floor of the format. We ran twelve public PX4 flight logs (four quadrotors, two
hexa/octorotors, three fixed wings, three VTOLs; \pxRecords{} in-flight records), chosen by a rule
fixed before any was opened, through the same encoder. The logs carry position at 5\,Hz, so the
comparison is at 0.2\,s spacing and above, not at the 50\,Hz of the operating point. At 0.2\,s a
real delta record averages \pxDelta\,B against the generator's \pxDeltaGen\,B, and a real
keyframe \pxKey\,B against \pxKeyGen. The spread is by speed: the six multicopters, mostly
hovering, average \pxDeltaSlow\,B and the three fixed wings up to \pxDeltaFast\,B. With real
sizes at 0.2\,s the design saves \pxSave\% (\pxSaveWorst\% in the least favourable log) where
the generator gives \pxSaveGen\%. The generator is representative to within about one
percentage point, and optimistic for fast vehicles.

For the operating rate itself we flew PX4 in software-in-the-loop --- its own estimator, logger
and MAVLink module on a simulated quadrotor --- with position logged every 20\,ms. Over
\sitlRecords{} in-flight records, at up to \sitlSpeedMax\,m/s, every delta record is
\deltaLean\,B, the floor of the format; the keyframe averages \sitlKey\,B and the design costs
\sitlBpr\,B per record, \sitlGenPct\% less than with the generator's records. The same
autopilot's 50\,Hz position stream holds a \sitlPeriod\,ms period to within
$\pm\sitlJitter$\,ms (central 68\%).

\subsection{Validation of the models}\label{sec:validation}
\textbf{Models against the simulator.} The broadcast model~\cite{machen2008} matches saturated
ns-3 runs within $\pm\valBcast$\% over $N=5$--50 (\seeds{} seeds).

\textbf{The access rule against two radios.} On two Raspberry~Pi~4 radios, one transmitter
alone loses \hwLossFrames{} frames in \hwSentFrames{} ($p = \hwLoss$). With both radios
saturated, \hwSatOff\% of the frames put on air are lost where the access rule gives
\hwSatStd\%, a prediction registered before the run. Below saturation the agreement is poorer:
as the medium fills from half to 85\% the radios lose \hwPoolLowRatio, \hwPoolMidRatio{} and
\hwPoolHighRatio{} times what the model predicts, and the registered prediction held at the
first two loads and failed at the third. The cause is not established. Below saturation a
radio often holds two frames of its own, and a radio of this family sends its queued frames
closer together than the standard allows --- one per
\hwCycleOn\,ms against \hwCycleStd\,ms --- as commercial cards are known
to~\cite{bianchi2007cards}. That state is common with two busy radios and rare at the operating
point. But a follow-up that switched off the vendor's frame bursting, with four further
predictions registered, failed all four: bursting has no demonstrated effect on the loss. No
capacity was measured: the capacities in this paper rest on simulation.

\textbf{Stream signing.} Table~\ref{tab:stream} places the schemes of Section~\ref{sec:related}
at our operating point. None is implemented here: each row is the lean one-record frame with
that scheme's authenticator in place of the signature. All of them
send a frame per record --- \strFpsSig~frames/s per node (\strFpsEmss{} for EMSS, with its
signature packets) where the design sends \strFpsDesign{} --- so the fixed channel time of a
frame is paid for every record whatever the size of the authenticator. A 13\,B tag in place of a 64\,B signature raises $N_{\max}$
from \strNmaxSig{} to \strNmaxMav{} nodes and gives up non-repudiation; four records in a
frame raise it to \strNmaxDesign. Where a packet does not verify alone the simulated figure
counts frames delivered, not records verified, and is an upper bound. Tree chaining is the one scheme that keeps what a ledger needs
--- every packet verifies alone and its origin can be shown to a third party --- and it pays \strAuthWl\,B per packet for
it. These schemes amortise the cost of signing; on a contended channel the cost that binds is
the frame.

\textbf{Aggregate signatures.} The nearest published comparators are certificateless aggregate signature schemes for vehicular
broadcast. Table~\ref{tab:clas} lists what one signer sends per message. For $n$ messages that is $n$
times as much, since each carries its own identity, key and signature components. Aggregation
acts afterwards, at a roadside unit: it reduces the verifier's work and what the unit forwards
--- in the one scheme read at source, 388\,B for a single signature and 784\,B for the
aggregate of a hundred~\cite{wang2022clas}. Batching acts before, on a sender's own records.
The two act on different links, and the table should not be read as a ranking:
those schemes provide conditional privacy, which AUTHBC does not; their group elements are
128\,B where our signature is 64\,B; and their figures are for a different message. What the
comparison does show is that per-message authentication of this kind costs several hundred
bytes, and that aggregation alone does not change that.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Capacities are simulated.} On hardware, contention was measured between two radios
only (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). The capacities in
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} come from ns-3 with one collision domain, equal received power and static
nodes. Unequal power with capture was simulated separately and raises them
(Sec.~\ref{sec:capacity}); hidden terminals, spatial reuse and mobility are absent. Send instants are
redrawn within each period (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). PX4's own stream, in simulation, holds its
period to about $\pm\sitlJitter$\,ms, which is nearer to the strictly periodic source and its
wider spread between runs. Three to
five radios are the next measurement; the model's predictions for them are registered in the
repository.

\textbf{Telemetry is synthetic.} Record sizes come from a generator. The check against flight
logs covers twelve logs of one autopilot at 5\,Hz position resolution; at 50\,Hz it rests on
one simulated flight of the same autopilot.

\textbf{One PHY, generated loss.} Capacity was simulated at 6\,Mb/s only. Both operating
points have $\Lambda D_{\max} = 5$ and so a batch of four; batches of two, three and eight were
simulated for the capacity rule alone, and nothing else here is evaluated at them. The loss
experiment
uses independent, Gilbert and length-dependent loss, all generated at the frame level, not a
channel trace; under the last, the design is below its target where the baseline's frame loses
5\% (Sec.~\ref{sec:loss}). The stream-signing schemes of Table~\ref{tab:stream} are sized
from their definitions and simulated as frames; none is implemented.

\textbf{Energy and CPU.} Only the sender is metered, with a sensor never checked against a
reference load; receiver and radio energy are modelled. The lean sender costs more energy per
record than the first format's. The receiver's CPU share counts verification and hashing, not
decoding; counting both, the prototype needs three of the board's four cores for the design's
neighbourhood, measured without a radio or a dispatcher in the path.

\textbf{Security scope.} Confidentiality, availability, key management and protection against a
delaying attacker are outside the design (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}), and the security argument is
informal. With one link per frame, continuity inside a frame rests on the frame signature alone,
and showing one record to a third party means showing its frame. The receiver therefore keeps
every accepted frame, at its on-air length for every $b$ records held.

\textbf{Post-quantum signatures.} An ML-DSA signature is 2420\,B~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v} and exceeds
the 1500\,B MTU with any header, so it cannot ride in one frame at any batch size; freshness caps
the batch at four, so it could not be amortised either. A post-quantum variant would be a
different design, built around fragmentation~\cite{ndss2024pqv2v,sobati2025fragmentsec}.

\textbf{Multi-hop.} Relayed records and more than one collision domain are not evaluated. A
relay cannot re-sign: it carries the originators' signatures or one BLS
aggregate~\cite{bgls2003}. Both are implemented; neither is measured here.

\section{Conclusion}
For small telemetry records the frame, not the record, is the unit at which authentication
should be costed, and what a frame must carry decides where it can be sent. A frame that verifies alone needs a header,
a chain link, a signature and one self-contained record: \exclCount{} of the twelve EU863-870 LoRaWAN
data rates cannot carry that at any batch size, \exclSigCount{} because the signature alone does
not fit and \exclFmtCount{} because of this frame's own header. Where the frame fits, one signature and one chain
link per four records cut the bytes per record by \saveLean\% and let one 802.11a collision
domain carry \nmaxLeanDesign{} nodes where per-record signing carries \nmaxLeanBase. Two
constraints shape the design: the batch is capped by freshness, not by the MTU, and a frame that depends on its predecessor does not meet a verifiability target equal
to the loss rate. Batching and header design decide what can run; among 64\,B signatures the
scheme does not. The capacity figures are simulated; on hardware the access rule behind them
held between two saturated radios and not at every load below saturation, and no capacity was
measured.
